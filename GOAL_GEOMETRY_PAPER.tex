\begin{abstract}
We study a deliberately austere quantum-control problem. An artificial agent
may choose a measurement and observe its classical outcome, but it is never
given the quantum state, Kraus operators, Born probabilities, Hamiltonian, or
tomographic estimates. Its experience is only a sequence of interventions and
consequences. The scientific objective is therefore broader than quantum
control: what operational representation of an unknown world can be learned
from first-person experience, and can many possible aims be organized into a
useful geometry?

We compare finite-history tabular Q-learning, goal-conditioned recurrent
Q-learning, and a predictive multi-goal gated recurrent unit (GRU). The full
agent jointly learns reward value, one-step outcome prediction, a
stochastic-shortest-path cost, and goal embeddings regularized by policy
similarity. Five environment families vary Hilbert-space dimension, hidden
initial state, measurement strength, informational completeness, and outcome
alphabet, including a qubit symmetric informationally complete (SIC)
measurement and mutually unbiased qutrit bases. Thirty-six agents are trained
across six scenarios, three backends, and two seeds.

The predictive geometry agent has the best macro-average success (80.5\%),
versus 79.1\% for finite-history Q-learning and 77.7\% for the reward-only GRU,
but no backend dominates every environment and neural seed variance is large.
Euclidean goal-embedding distance correlates moderately with held-out policy
distance (mean Spearman $\rho=0.54$) and more weakly with independent
trajectory distance ($\rho=0.31$). Learned reachability is directionally
informative but not metrically calibrated: blank-history cost correlates
$r=0.47$ with empirical conditional hitting time and has mean absolute error
1.75 interventions. Intervention maps nevertheless reveal a legible directed
structure in which outcomes reduce distance to matching goals while often
increasing distance to alternatives. Goal geometry is thus a productive
diagnostic and planning language, but its metric interpretation requires
stronger validation.

We therefore perform a second, inverse-designed study aimed directly at
emergent space. A nine-dimensional system supports nine place goals and eight
movement instruments. Optimizing diagonal-move success selects $p_d=0.715$,
near $1/\sqrt2$, and gives exact 2D stress 0.0365. Three coordinate-free
Q-learning agents achieve 100\% all-pairs navigation; their learned hitting
costs have 1D/2D/3D stresses $0.372/0.071/0.064$ and recover the concealed
$3\times3$ arrangement with Procrustes $R^2=0.975$. Partial-observability and
Manhattan controls identify operational localization and isotropic action cost
as necessary ingredients. This constitutes a limited existence proof for
stochastic motion in an agent's emergent hodological space and motivates a
spatial-base/internal-fiber research program.

Finally, we remove the exact online place report. Four individually ambiguous
binary quantum-nondemolition beacons provide a 48-outcome scan history; a GRU
predicts a delayed terminal landmark, and an empirically learned transition
model supports belief-state planning through blind movements. Across three
seeds, delayed-landmark accuracy is $0.964\pm0.008$ and held-out all-pairs
navigation is $0.973\pm0.004$, compared with $1.000\pm0.001$ for an online
localization oracle. Policy costs have 2D stress $0.075\pm0.005$, correlate
$0.948\pm0.013$ with exact stochastic costs, and recover concealed coordinates
with Procrustes $R^2=0.987\pm0.005$. Equal-budget short-memory and
place-independent-sensor controls fail. This predictive atlas strengthens the
existence result: place can be an anticipated future experience maintained
from history rather than a supplied current symbol. Delayed landmark
supervision, landmark-anchored surveys, commuting sensors, and expensive fixed
scans remain explicit limitations.

We then make sensing itself goal directed. Open-boundary pilots expose a
boundary-homing loophole, motivating local random-unitary layer swaps whose
doubly stochastic kernels preserve uncertainty and the optimized 2D costs.
Beacon, movement, and commitment actions all cost one. An atlas-preserving
active policy chooses informative probes and uses $19.12\pm0.53$ total
interventions rather than the fixed scan's $50.49\pm0.02$, while achieving
$0.965\pm0.004$ success, movement stress $0.111\pm0.006$, and Procrustes
$R^2=0.987\pm0.004$. A cheaper goal-relative value-of-information state loses
global metric fidelity. Separating movement from sensing cost produces a first
operational spatial-base/epistemic-fiber visualization: beliefs move in the
learned plane while entropy records an internal height. This is a precursor,
not yet a mathematical fiber bundle.

The final study asks whether nine orthogonal place states were ever necessary.
They were not. Two rationally independent phase controls on a single qubit
give nine nonorthogonal sequence-labelled goals with an exact open
$3\times3$ Manhattan word metric. Unit-cost retry instruments realize exact
Euclidean distance in expectation, although the encoding is dense on one
Bloch circle and develops severe finite-resolution shortcuts. A qutrit removes
that dimensional pathology: two commuting phase generators have isotropic
Fubini--Study covariance, a 121-state finite orbit, and an exact Euclidean
nine-goal chart. An independent Hesse-SIC construction makes the goals common
measurement outcomes and has the analytic Bellman solution
$V_g(s)=6+d_{\mathbb Z_3^2}(s,g)$. Its geometry is a two-dimensional torus,
not a Euclidean plane. These examples establish that Hilbert dimension does
not count goals, separate physical, predictive, and automaton memory, and give
a finite necessary-and-sufficient test: Bellman realizability together with
the Schoenberg positive-semidefinite rank criterion. They also sharpen the
open problem from whether low-dimensional systems can carry space to why
physically natural instruments should select a robust Euclidean norm.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Quantum control is usually described from the standpoint of an external
modeler, who writes down a state $\rho$, a Hamiltonian, and control or
measurement operators before optimizing a protocol. Model-free quantum control
instead asks what can be learned through interaction, an approach already used
for quantum feedback, state preparation, and error correction
\cite{fosel2018,sivak2022,borah2021}.

This work imposes a stricter informational boundary. The agent chooses an
intervention $a_t$ and receives a classical outcome $o_t$. It is not given
$\rho_t$, measurement operators, expectation values, fidelities, or
tomography. Its information at time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
 h_t=(a_1,o_1,\ldots,a_{t-1},o_{t-1}). \label{eq:history}
\end{equation}
This interface is compatible with QBism's emphasis on an agent's actions,
experiences, and expectations \cite{fuchs2014}, although the algorithms do not
assume or establish any interpretation of quantum theory. Agent-centered here
describes the information boundary.

The central difficulty is partial observability. An external physicist may use
$\rho_t$ as a Markov state, but the learner cannot. It needs an internal
summary $z_t=F(h_t)$ retaining distinctions relevant to prediction and control.
This motivates three objects that must remain distinct:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{predictive state}: which histories imply the same future
 intervention/outcome statistics;
 \item \textbf{strategic goal geometry}: which goals call for similar policies
 or trajectories;
 \item \textbf{directed reachability}: how difficult a goal is from the
 agent's present history.
\end{enumerate}
Our methodological contribution is a validation framework comparing learned
Euclidean goal embeddings with held-out policy behavior, trajectory
signatures, empirical hitting times, finite-horizon success curves, and
intervention-induced changes in all goal distances.

The paper is written to serve two audiences. Readers primarily interested in
reinforcement learning may regard the quantum simulator as an unusual partially
observed environment and begin with \cref{sec:rl}. Readers primarily interested
in quantum foundations may use \cref{sec:quantum} to see exactly which
quantities are available to the external simulator and which are withheld from
the agent. No prior knowledge of reinforcement learning is assumed: we derive
the value functions, Bellman recursions, temporal-difference update, recurrent
state construction, and evaluation criteria used below. Likewise, the spatial
study in \cref{sec:spatial} derives the Kraus instruments, exact hitting costs,
multidimensional-scaling test, and every reported geometric diagnostic.
The successor in \cref{sec:predictive-atlas} then derives weak-beacon
localization, delayed predictive supervision, Bayesian belief filtering, and
model-based navigation without an exact online place symbol.
The active extension in \cref{sec:active-atlas} prices sensing and motion in
common units, repairs a boundary-homing confound with coherent reversible
channels, and tests a provisional spatial-base/epistemic-fiber decomposition.
Finally, \cref{sec:lowdim} removes the nine-dimensional place register. It
derives exact low-dimensional constructions, proves the relevant obstruction
and embedding criteria, and distinguishes a geometry carried by quantum
dynamics from one stored only in a goal automaton.

The stronger motivation is hodological: for an agent, goals that require few
reliable interventions are near and difficult goals are far. Lewin introduced
path-structured ``life space'' as a formal language for goal-directed behavior
\cite{lewin1936}. Such a space is generally directed and high-dimensional.
Familiar physical space would be a special regime in which a large family of
place-like goals admits a common low-stress 2D or 3D embedding and learned
action histories become trajectories through it. We treat that as an inverse
problem over initial states, Kraus instruments, observation coarse-grainings,
and goal repertoires, not as an assumption.

It is useful to state the logical order of the claim at the outset. The hidden
simulator defines quantum transitions; interaction produces an empirical
control problem; successful control defines all-pairs goal costs; those costs
are tested for low-dimensional Euclidean structure; and only then are learned
policy histories plotted as spatial trajectories. A visually attractive plot
at the last stage cannot substitute for competence or metric validation at the
earlier stages.

\subsection*{Notation and reading guide}

Upper-case random variables such as $S_t,A_t,O_t$ distinguish random
quantities from realizations $s_t,a_t,o_t$. A policy is denoted $\pi$; the
expectation and probability under it are $\E_\pi$ and $\Prob_\pi$. The hidden
quantum state is $\rho_t$, the observable interaction history is $h_t$, and a
learned or hand-constructed operational state is $z_t=F(h_t)$. Reward value is
$Q$; positive intervention cost is $C$ or $d$; a goal is $g$; and an embedding
coordinate is $x_i$. The word \emph{state} is consequently qualified whenever
ambiguity matters: quantum state, Markov state, recurrent state, or goal
progress state. Distances between goals are symmetric only when explicitly
symmetrized. This distinction is central because goal reachability is generally
directed.

\section{Hidden quantum dynamics}
\label{sec:quantum}

\subsection{Quantum instruments and backaction}

A finite-dimensional Hilbert space $\mathcal H\simeq\mathbb C^d$ is the
external modeler's state space. A pure state is a unit ray represented by
$|\psi\rangle$; uncertainty or classical mixing is represented by a density
operator $\rho$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
 \rho=\rho^\dagger,\qquad \rho\succeq0,\qquad \Tr\rho=1.
\end{equation}
The notation $\rho\succeq0$ means
$\langle\phi|\rho|\phi\rangle\geq0$ for every $|\phi\rangle$. A pure state has
$\rho=|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|$ and $\Tr\rho^2=1$; a genuinely mixed state
has $\Tr\rho^2<1$. These facts are used by the simulator and its tests, but
never supplied as features to the learner.

A measurement action is more completely described by a \emph{quantum
instrument} than by a probability distribution. For action $a$ and observed
outcome $o$, let $\mathcal I_o^{(a)}$ be a completely positive,
trace-nonincreasing map. A Kraus representation is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I_o^{(a)}(\rho)=\sum_{\mu}
 K_{o\mu}^{(a)}\rho K_{o\mu}^{(a)\dagger}.
 \label{eq:instrument-map}
\end{equation}
The index $\mu$ labels physical branches that need not be distinguished by the
classical apparatus. Trace preservation after outcomes are forgotten requires
\cite{kraus1983,nielsen2010}
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{o,\mu} K_{o\mu}^{(a)\dagger}K_{o\mu}^{(a)}=I. \label{eq:complete}
\end{equation}
The positive operators
$E_o^{(a)}=\sum_\mu K_{o\mu}^{(a)\dagger}K_{o\mu}^{(a)}$ form a POVM, because
$E_o^{(a)}\succeq0$ and $\sum_oE_o^{(a)}=I$. They determine outcome
probabilities but not, by themselves, the post-measurement state. The latter
depends on the full instrument. The hidden simulator samples and updates by
\begin{align}
 p(o|\rho,a)&=\Tr\!\left[\mathcal I_o^{(a)}(\rho)\right]
             =\Tr(E_o^{(a)}\rho), \label{eq:born}\\
 \rho'&=\frac{\mathcal I_o^{(a)}(\rho)}{p(o|\rho,a)}. \label{eq:update}
\end{align}
The normalization follows immediately because
$\Tr\rho'=\Tr\mathcal I_o^{(a)}(\rho)/p(o|\rho,a)=1$. Completeness gives
$\sum_op(o|\rho,a)=\Tr\rho=1$. These elementary checks matter computationally:
failure of either condition would turn apparent learning behavior into a
simulator artifact.

Measurement therefore does two logically separate things. It produces an
experience $o$, which may inform the agent, and it changes the hidden state,
which changes future outcome statistics. The second effect is measurement
backaction. If action $a$ makes goal $g$ easy but destroys the preparation
needed for goal $g'$, then going from $g'$ toward $g$ need not have the same
cost as returning. This is the operational origin of directed reachability in
\cref{sec:geometry}. The learner never evaluates
\cref{eq:instrument-map,eq:born,eq:update}; it receives only the action label it
chose and the sampled outcome label.

\subsection{Projective, unsharp, and informationally complete measurements}

For a projective basis $\{|b_o\rangle\}$, one may take
$K_o=|b_o\rangle\langle b_o|$. An immediate repetition returns the same
outcome with certainty, whereas a measurement in an incompatible basis can
erase that certainty. The qubit worlds use Pauli $Z$, $X$, and $Y$ bases. For
example, $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$ are $Z$ eigenstates and
$|\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$ are $X$ eigenstates, so
$|\langle0|+\rangle|^2=1/2$. An $X$ measurement after a known $Z$ outcome is
therefore uniformly random and prepares a new $X$ eigenstate. Their
noncommutativity creates incompatible preparation strategies and makes action
order relevant.

The unsharp binary instruments use axis projectors $P_+,P_-$ and strength
$q\in(1/2,1)$:
\begin{align}
 K_0&=\sqrt q\,P_+ +\sqrt{1-q}\,P_-,&
 K_1&=\sqrt{1-q}\,P_+ +\sqrt q\,P_- . \label{eq:weak}
\end{align}
Because $P_+P_-=0$ and $P_++P_-=I$,
$K_0^\dagger K_0+K_1^\dagger K_1=I$. At $q=0.5$, both effects equal $I/2$:
the outcome is uninformative and the state change is correspondingly weak. As
$q\to1$, the operation approaches a projective measurement. We use $q=0.8$ to
create repeated, noisy evidence rather than one-shot certainty.

A SIC-POVM in dimension $d$ contains $d^2$ equiangular rank-one effects and is
informationally complete \cite{renes2004}. For the qubit tetrahedral SIC,
\begin{equation}
 E_i=\frac{I+\bm r_i\cdot\bm\sigma}{4},\qquad \sum_{i=0}^3E_i=I,
\end{equation}
where $\bm r_i$ are tetrahedral Bloch vectors; we use the positive square-root
instrument $K_i=\sqrt{E_i}$. This tests mixed outcome alphabets: projective
actions have two outcomes while the SIC has four. Invalid logits are masked.
Informational completeness means that the probabilities
$p_i=\Tr(E_i\rho)$ determine $\rho$ uniquely for an external observer with
enough identically prepared samples. It does \emph{not} mean that a single
outcome reveals the state, nor does it remove backaction. In the present online
problem the learner receives one outcome and the same system continues from
the resulting conditional state.

Two bases $\{|e_j\rangle\}$ and $\{|f_k\rangle\}$ are mutually unbiased when
$|\langle e_j|f_k\rangle|^2=1/d$ for all $j,k$. Certainty in one becomes a
uniform distribution in the other. MUBs formalize complementarity and support
tomography \cite{durt2010}. The qutrit world uses three projective MUBs.

\subsection{From hidden quantum state to an observable control process}

Combining \cref{eq:born,eq:update} over time yields a classical distribution
over complete interaction histories. If the initial state is $\rho_0$ and a
fixed action sequence is $a_{1:T}$, then the probability of outcomes
$o_{1:T}$ is
\begin{equation}
 \Prob(o_{1:T}\mid a_{1:T},\rho_0)
 =\Tr\!\left(M_T\rho_0M_T^\dagger\right),\qquad
 M_T=K_{o_T}^{(a_T)}\cdots K_{o_1}^{(a_1)},
 \label{eq:history-prob}
\end{equation}
for one Kraus operator per observed outcome; with coarse-grained branches one
sums the corresponding terms. A policy makes the actions themselves depend on
past outcomes. The result is an adaptive experiment: by selecting $a_t$ as a
function of $h_t$, the agent changes both what it can learn and what future it
creates.

Two hidden density operators are operationally indistinguishable to this agent
when they induce the same distribution for every experiment constructible from
the available action repertoire. Thus the physically convenient coordinate
$\rho$ may contain distinctions irrelevant to the agent, while a short history
may omit distinctions essential for future control. The representation problem
is to find the quotient between these extremes: enough memory to predict and
act, without importing the simulator's private state description.

\section{Reinforcement learning from first principles}
\label{sec:rl}

\subsection{The agent--environment loop}

Reinforcement learning (RL) studies how an agent can improve sequential
decisions from experience rather than from a supplied transition model
\cite{sutton2018}. At time $t$ the agent has some information state $S_t$, draws
an action $A_t$ from a policy, the environment produces reward $R_{t+1}$ and a
new information state $S_{t+1}$, and the cycle repeats. An episode is a random
trajectory
\begin{equation}
 \Xi=(S_0,A_0,R_1,S_1,A_1,R_2,\ldots,S_T).
 \label{eq:rl-trajectory}
\end{equation}
The essential difficulty is delayed consequence. A measurement may have small
or negative immediate reward yet prepare a state from which the desired
outcome becomes likely several interventions later. Assigning credit only to
the final action would miss the preparation strategy.

A policy $\pi(a|s)$ is a conditional probability distribution over actions:
$\pi(a|s)\geq0$ and $\sum_a\pi(a|s)=1$. A deterministic policy is the special
case in which one action has probability one. During training we distinguish
the \emph{behavior policy}, which generates data and deliberately explores,
from the \emph{target policy}, whose value is being estimated. At evaluation
time exploration is disabled so that performance measures the learned
decision rule rather than random experimentation.

\subsection{Markov decision processes}

A finite Markov decision process (MDP) is a tuple
\begin{equation}
 (\mathcal S,\mathcal A,p,r,\gamma),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal S$ and $\mathcal A$ are state and action sets,
\begin{equation}
 p(s',r\mid s,a)
 =\Prob(S_{t+1}=s',R_{t+1}=r\mid S_t=s,A_t=a)
 \label{eq:mdp-kernel}
\end{equation}
is the time-homogeneous transition/reward kernel, and
$\gamma\in[0,1]$ is a discount factor. The Markov property asserts that once
$S_t$ and $A_t$ are known, earlier history supplies no further information
about the next transition. It is a sufficiency claim, not a claim that the
state is directly visible or ontologically fundamental.

The discounted return from time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
 G_t=\sum_{k=0}^{T-t-1}\gamma^kR_{t+k+1}
 =R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}.
 \label{eq:return}
\end{equation}
Discounting makes near rewards count more strongly and ensures a finite sum in
continuing problems with bounded rewards. In finite episodic problems it also
expresses a preference for earlier completion. It should not be confused with
the undiscounted intervention count used later for hodological distance.

For a fixed policy, the state and action values are
\begin{align}
 V^\pi(s)&=\E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s],\\
 Q^\pi(s,a)&=\E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s,A_t=a].
 \label{eq:value-defs}
\end{align}
Conditioning on the first transition and substituting
\cref{eq:return} gives the Bellman expectation equations:
\begin{align}
 V^\pi(s)
 &=\sum_a\pi(a|s)\sum_{s',r}p(s',r|s,a)
       [r+\gamma V^\pi(s')],\\
 Q^\pi(s,a)
 &=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r|s,a)
       \left[r+\gamma\sum_{a'}\pi(a'|s')Q^\pi(s',a')\right].
 \label{eq:bellman-expectation}
\end{align}
These are consistency equations: the value of the whole future equals the
one-step reward plus the value of the remaining future.

The optimal values $V^*(s)=\max_\pi V^\pi(s)$ and
$Q^*(s,a)=\max_\pi Q^\pi(s,a)$ satisfy Bellman's principle of optimality
\cite{bellman1957}:
\begin{align}
 V^*(s)&=\max_a\sum_{s',r}p(s',r|s,a)[r+\gamma V^*(s')],\\
 Q^*(s,a)&=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r|s,a)
 [r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a')]. \label{eq:bellman}
\end{align}
Once $Q^*$ is known, any maximizing action is optimal. Dynamic programming
would solve these equations using a known $p$. Model-free RL instead estimates
their solution from sampled transitions.

\subsection{From Monte Carlo returns to temporal differences}

One could wait until an episode ends and use the observed $G_t$ as a noisy
target for $Q(S_t,A_t)$. This Monte Carlo estimator is unbiased for the value
of the behavior policy, but it has high variance and delays learning. A
temporal-difference (TD) method instead \emph{bootstraps}: it uses a current
estimate of the remaining value before the episode ends. For Q-learning the
one-step target and TD error are
\begin{align}
 Y_t^Q&=R_{t+1}+\gamma(1-D_{t+1})max_{a'}Q(S_{t+1},a'),\\
 \delta_t^Q&=Y_t^Q-Q(S_t,A_t),
 \label{eq:td-error}
\end{align}
where $D_{t+1}=1$ indicates termination and prevents value from leaking beyond
the episode. The tabular update is
\begin{equation}
 Q(S_t,A_t)\leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t)+\alpha\delta_t^Q.
 \label{eq:qlearn}
\end{equation}
Thus $\alpha\in(0,1]$ controls how far the old estimate moves toward the new
sample target. The update is a stochastic approximation to
\cref{eq:bellman} \cite{watkins1992}.

Q-learning is \emph{off-policy}: the maximum in \cref{eq:td-error} evaluates a
greedy continuation even when the behavior policy chose a non-greedy action.
In our epsilon-greedy behavior policy,
\begin{equation}
 \pi_\epsilon(a|s)=
 \begin{cases}
  1-\epsilon+\epsilon/|\mathcal A|,&
     a\in\argmax_bQ(s,b)\quad\text{(with tie handling)},\\
  \epsilon/|\mathcal A|,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:epsilon-greedy}
\end{equation}
Large early $\epsilon$ discovers interventions whose long-term value was
initially unknown; decay later exploits accumulated knowledge. Too little
exploration leaves goals unvisited, whereas too much late exploration obscures
competence and lengthens paths.

For a finite tabular MDP, bounded rewards, sufficient visitation, and learning
rates satisfying standard stochastic-approximation conditions, Q-learning
converges to $Q^*$ with probability one \cite{watkins1992}. Fixed learning
rates, finite data, partial observability, and nonlinear recurrent
approximation weaken these guarantees. Our results are therefore empirical and
must be checked across held-out episodes and seeds.

\subsection{Goal conditioning and sparse success signals}

Ordinary RL learns one reward problem. Here a single agent faces a family of
goals $g\in\mathcal G$. Goal conditioning augments the information state and
value function:
\begin{equation}
 Q^\pi(s,g,a)=\E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s,G=g,A_t=a].
 \label{eq:goal-conditioned-q}
\end{equation}
This permits experience about common dynamics to be shared while retaining
different policies for different aims. It also makes geometry meaningful: the
goals are compared through behavior learned in the same environment and model,
rather than through unrelated specialist agents.

Sparse terminal rewards are conceptually clean but hard to discover by random
exploration. The sequence experiments therefore add checkpoint rewards and a
small per-step penalty. Reward shaping changes the finite-data learning
problem, so success and unshaped intervention counts are still evaluated
directly. In the spatial experiment all goals have the same terminal form and
the central geometric quantity is not discounted return; it is empirical
first-passage cost after training.

\subsection{Partial observability, beliefs, and histories}

In a partially observable MDP (POMDP), the environment has a latent Markov
state $X_t$, but the agent observes only $O_t$ generated from it
\cite{kaelbling1998}. The exact Bayesian information state is the belief
\begin{equation}
 b_t(x)=\Prob(X_t=x\mid h_t),
 \label{eq:belief}
\end{equation}
which is a probability distribution over hidden states. Given a known model,
the belief update combines prediction under the chosen action with Bayes'
rule. In our setting the quantum state and instrument are unknown, so the agent
cannot perform that privileged update. It must act from history,
$\pi(a_t|h_t,g)$, or learn a compressed substitute.

The finite-history baseline uses
\begin{equation}
 s_t^{(L)}=(g,j_t,(a_{t-L},o_{t-L}),\ldots,(a_{t-1},o_{t-1})),
 \label{eq:tabstate}
\end{equation}
padding short histories at episode start. This representation is transparent
and works well when the relevant memory is genuinely short. Its number of
possible suffixes grows roughly as $(|\mathcal A||\mathcal O|)^L$, however,
and two histories share statistical strength only if their suffixes are
identical. A long literal suffix can also be \emph{too discriminating}: it may
treat two routes to the same operational place as different states. That issue
becomes decisive in \cref{sec:spatial-failures}.

\subsection{Predictive state as an operational equivalence class}

Predictive-state representations replace beliefs about a privileged latent
variable by predictions of observable future tests \cite{littman2001}. A test
$q=(a_1,o_1,\ldots,a_k,o_k)$ asks for the probability that outcomes
$o_{1:k}$ occur if interventions $a_{1:k}$ are imposed. Define
\begin{equation}
 h\sim_{\rm pred}h'
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \Prob(q\mid h)=\Prob(q\mid h')\ \text{for every feasible future test }q.
 \label{eq:predictive-equivalence}
\end{equation}
Histories in the same equivalence class have identical future affordances and
need not be distinguished for prediction or control. This is precisely the
agent-centered notion of state needed here: it is defined by possible future
experience, not by access to $\rho$.

Exact predictive equivalence is demanding because there are arbitrarily long
tests. We approximate it with a recurrent vector $z_t=F_\theta(h_t)$ trained
jointly for reward and one-step outcome prediction. One-step accuracy alone
does not prove sufficiency for long-horizon control; it is an inductive bias and
diagnostic. Stronger future work should test multi-step predictions and whether
histories with similar $z$ remain interchangeable under novel intervention
sequences.

\subsection{GRU memory and function approximation}

A recurrent network recursively maintains fixed-size memory
\begin{equation}
 z_{t+1}=F_\theta(z_t,a_t,o_t),\qquad z_0=\bm0.
 \label{eq:memory}
\end{equation}
With $x_t$ the learned embedding of the action/outcome pair, a gated recurrent
unit (GRU) \cite{cho2014} can be written
\begin{align}
 r_t&=\sigma(W_rx_t+U_rz_t+b_r),&
 u_t&=\sigma(W_ux_t+U_uz_t+b_u),\\
 \widetilde z_{t+1}&=\tanh(W_hx_t+U_h(r_t\odot z_t)+b_h),&
 z_{t+1}&=(1-u_t)\odot\widetilde z_{t+1}+u_t\odot z_t.
 \label{eq:gru}
\end{align}
Here $\sigma(v)=1/(1+e^{-v})$ is applied coordinatewise and $\odot$ denotes
coordinatewise multiplication. The reset gate $r_t$ controls which previous
features enter the candidate memory; the update gate $u_t$ interpolates between
old and candidate memory. Unlike a fixed suffix, the same vector can learn to
retain an old decisive outcome while forgetting many irrelevant recent events.

The value table is replaced by a neural function $Q_\theta(z,g,j,a)$. Given
target $Y_t^Q$, the squared TD loss is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_Q(\theta)=\frac1B\sum_{t\in\mathcal B}
 [Y_t^Q-Q_\theta(z_t,g,j_t,a_t)]^2.
 \label{eq:q-loss}
\end{equation}
The target depends on a second parameter vector $\theta^-$, held fixed while
differentiating the loss, because otherwise the network would chase a target
that moves in the same gradient step. Following deep Q-learning practice
\cite{mnih2015}, it is updated softly:
\begin{equation}
 \theta^-\leftarrow(1-\tau)\theta^-+\tau\theta.
 \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
Complete episodes are unrolled and replayed so losses at late time steps can
alter earlier memory updates through backpropagation through time. Gradient
clipping limits rare unstable updates. None of these devices restores the
tabular convergence theorem; they are practical stabilizers.

\subsection{How an RL claim should be evaluated}

An RL result combines at least three random sources: stochastic environment
outcomes, exploratory action choices, and parameter initialization or data
order. Training also changes its own data distribution. Consequently, a single
learning curve is not a stable estimate of an algorithm's expected
performance. Reproducibility studies document ranking reversals and unreliable
uncertainty estimates from small seed counts \cite{henderson2018,agarwal2021}.

We separate the following questions.
\begin{description}[style=nextline]
 \item[Did the policy achieve the task?]
 Held-out success probability estimates
 $\Prob_\pi(\tau_g\leq T)$. It is the primary competence measure.
 \item[How quickly did successful trials finish?]
 Conditional mean duration estimates
 $\E[\tau_g\mid\tau_g\leq T]$. It must never be read alone, because a policy
 that succeeds only on easy random outcomes can look artificially fast.
 \item[How does reliability accumulate with time?]
 The curve $T\mapsto\Prob(\tau_g\leq T)$ reveals whether two policies with the
 same eventual success differ at useful deadlines.
 \item[Did a representation acquire the intended meaning?]
 Embeddings are compared with held-out policy and trajectory distances rather
 than judged by appearance. Learned costs are compared with empirical hitting
 times rather than accepted as distances by construction.
 \item[Does the conclusion survive nuisance variation?]
 We report environments, goals, and random seeds separately before aggregates.
 Sample standard deviations describe the observed runs but, with two or three
 seeds, do not provide precise population uncertainty.
\end{description}

Finite horizons create right censoring: failure tells us only that
$\tau_g>T$, not its actual value. One response is a restricted mean
$\E[\min(\tau_g,T)]$, which keeps failures in the quantity but depends on the
chosen horizon. Another is to report the entire success curve. We use both
ideas where appropriate and explicitly label conditional quantities. These
evaluation distinctions will later prevent geometric quality from being
confused with mere navigation success.

\section{Sequence goals and three geometries}
\label{sec:geometry}

The word \emph{geometry} is used in three related but nonidentical senses. A
strategic geometry compares two goals with each other. A trajectory geometry
compares the long-run behaviors used to pursue them. A reachability geometry
compares the current operational state with each goal. The first two are
symmetric dissimilarities; the third is naturally directed. Keeping these
objects separate prevents a common category error: two goals can require
similar actions without either being easy from the present state.

\subsection{Goals as finite-state recognition problems}

A sequence goal is an ordered list of action/outcome checkpoints
\begin{equation}
 g=((a_1^g,o_1^g),\ldots,(a_{m_g}^g,o_{m_g}^g)).
 \label{eq:sequence-goal}
\end{equation}
Other events may occur between checkpoints. Progress $J_t\in\{0,\ldots,m_g\}$
is the number of checkpoints already matched. It updates deterministically:
\begin{equation}
 J_{t+1}=J_t+\mathbb 1\{(A_t,O_t)=(a_{J_t+1}^g,o_{J_t+1}^g)\},
 \label{eq:progress-update}
\end{equation}
with $J=m_g$ absorbing. Thus each goal defines a small deterministic automaton
driven by observable experiences. Including $J_t$ in the agent's input does not
leak quantum state; it is a lossless computation from history and the stated
task.

Rewards are 10 for completion, 1 for an intermediate checkpoint, and $-0.01$
otherwise. The completion bonus emphasizes success, checkpoint reward helps
credit assignment, and step penalty weakly prefers shorter solutions. A
goal-conditioned value $Q(z,g,j,a)$ lets the same remembered history support
different actions for different desired futures. The spatial study later uses
a simpler family of one-checkpoint goals, all expressed through the same probe
action.

\subsection{What properties make a distance?}

A mathematical metric $d$ obeys nonnegativity, identity of indiscernibles,
symmetry, and the triangle inequality:
\begin{equation}
 d(x,y)\geq0,\quad d(x,y)=0\Leftrightarrow x=y,\quad
 d(x,y)=d(y,x),\quad d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z).
 \label{eq:metric-axioms}
\end{equation}
Operational goal costs need not satisfy every axiom. Distinct goals can be
behaviorally interchangeable; quantum backaction can violate symmetry; finite
sampling and imperfect policies can violate the triangle inequality. We use
\emph{geometry} broadly for structured relations among goals, while reserving
\emph{Euclidean-compatible metric} for a symmetric matrix that is well
represented by ordinary Euclidean distances. Section~\ref{sec:spatial}
subjects that stronger claim to explicit tests.

\subsection{Symmetric strategic similarity}

Each known goal has embedding $e_g\in\R^k$. Distance
$D_E(g,h)=\|e_g-e_h\|_2$ is intended to mean similarity of strategy, not labels.
At encountered state $z$, define
\begin{equation}
 \pi_g(a|z,j)=\softmax_a(Q(z,g,j,a)/T). \label{eq:policy}
\end{equation}
For policies $p,q$, Jensen--Shannon divergence is
\begin{equation}
 D_{\rm KL}(p\|q)=\sum_a p(a)\log\frac{p(a)}{q(a)},\qquad
 \JS(p,q)=\tfrac12D_{\rm KL}(p\|m)+\tfrac12D_{\rm KL}(q\|m),
 \quad m=\tfrac12(p+q).
\end{equation}
Mixing through $m$ keeps the Jensen--Shannon divergence finite when one policy
assigns zero probability to an action favored by the other. It is symmetric,
bounded, and its square root is a metric \cite{endres2003}. If two goals induce
nearly identical soft action preferences at many encountered states, they are
strategically close even when their textual labels differ. The regularizer is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_{\rm geom}=\frac1{|\mathcal P|}\sum_{g<h}
 [\|e_g-e_h\|_2-c\sqrt{\JS(\pi_g,\pi_h)}]^2. \label{eq:geoloss}
\end{equation}
where $c$ sets units and $\mathcal P$ is a sampled set of goal pairs. The policy
target is detached: gradients change embeddings to reflect current policy
dissimilarity but do not distort the policy merely to make the geometry easy.
Geometry is nevertheless partly imposed by this loss. Agreement on held-out
states tests whether that inductive bias captures persistent strategy; it is
not evidence of spontaneous emergence without bias.

\subsection{Trajectory similarity}

Local policy similarity may miss long-horizon differences. From held-out
rollouts we build
\begin{equation}
 \phi(g)=\bigl[\widehat f_g(a),\widehat f_g(a,o),
 \widehat{\E}(\tau_g/T),\widehat{\Prob}(\tau_g\leq T)\bigr]
 \label{eq:trajectory-signature}
\end{equation}
and $D_{\rm traj}(g,h)=\|\phi(g)-\phi(h)\|_2$. This resembles successor
features, which describe policies by expected future features and support
transfer \cite{barreto2017}, although our signature is diagnostic rather than a
learned successor model.

The distinction is important. $D_{\rm policy}$ compares action distributions
at a common collection of local states. $D_{\rm traj}$ compares the visitation
distributions those policies actually induce over complete rollouts. Two goals
may choose the same first action and then diverge; conversely, different local
choices may lead to similar aggregate frequencies. Correlation with both
quantities is stronger evidence than correlation with the training target
alone.

\subsection{Directed reachability and displacement}

Let $\tau_g$ be first completion time. The ideal intervention-count distance is
\begin{equation}
 \tau_g=\inf\{k\geq0:J_{t+k}=m_g\},\qquad
 d^*(z,g,j)=\min_\pi\E_\pi[\tau_g|z,j]. \label{eq:hitting}
\end{equation}
This is a stochastic shortest-path problem \cite{bertsekas1991} with Bellman
form
\begin{equation}
 d^*(z,g,j)=
 \begin{cases}
 0,&j=m_g,\\
 1+\displaystyle\min_a\sum_oP(o|z,a)
 d^*(F(z,a,o),g,j'(a,o)),&j<m_g.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:costbellman}
\end{equation}
Every nonterminal intervention contributes one unit; the expectation averages
over measurement outcomes; and minimization chooses the best first action.
Unlike discounted return, this quantity is expressed directly in
interventions. It can be infinite when a goal is unreachable, which is why
finite-horizon empirical estimates require careful failure handling.

The network learns positive action cost $C(z,g,j,a)$ and sets
$d(z,g,j)=\min_a C(z,g,j,a)$. Its target is 1 on completion and otherwise
\begin{equation}
 y_t^C=1+\min_{a'}C_{\theta^-}(z_{t+1},g,j_{t+1},a'). \label{eq:costtarget}
\end{equation}
This target bootstraps exactly as Q-learning does, but minimizes positive cost
rather than maximizing reward. It is directed: measurement can destroy
conditions needed to return. It is also policy-sensitive in finite data,
because samples come from reward-oriented exploratory behavior rather than a
separate cost-optimal policy.

For $N$ goals, the agent's goal-relative position is
\begin{equation}
 \bm D(z)=(d(z,g_1),\ldots,d(z,g_N)).
\end{equation}
An observed intervention produces displacement
$\Delta\bm D_t=\bm D(z_{t+1})-\bm D(z_t)$. Negative coordinates mean closer,
positive farther. With the learned predictor, a contemplated action has
\begin{equation}
 \E[d'_g|z,a]=\sum_o\widehat P(o|z,a)d(F(z,a,o),g), \label{eq:planning}
\end{equation}
which provides a route to model-based planning.

The vector $\bm D(z)$ behaves like an agent-centered coordinate chart: it says
where the present lies relative to a fixed landmark set of goals. These are
not Cartesian coordinates. They can be redundant, curved, asymmetric, and
high-dimensional. If sufficiently many landmark distances obey a common low-
dimensional Euclidean model, one may subsequently recover Cartesian-like
coordinates by multidimensional scaling. That additional step is the central
subject of \cref{sec:spatial}.

A scalar mean hides risk, so we also estimate
\begin{equation}
 R_g(z,T)=\Prob(\tau_g\le T|z,\pi_g). \label{eq:curve}
\end{equation}
This parallels distributional RL's emphasis on distributions rather than only
expected return \cite{bellemare2017}.

For example, a deterministic three-step route and a one-step gamble that
succeeds with probability one half and otherwise takes a long detour can have
similar means but very different deadline reliability. A full first-passage
distribution distinguishes them. The present work records empirical success
curves; learning calibrated distributions directly is left to future work.

\section{Architecture and experimental protocol}
\label{sec:methods}

All agents share one interaction protocol and receive only goal ID, progress,
action, outcome, reward, and termination. They never receive a density matrix,
state fidelity, target-state overlap, Born probability, or hidden coordinate.
This common interface makes architectural comparisons meaningful: differences
come from memory and auxiliary learning objectives rather than extra physical
information.

\subsection{Three learner families}

The \textbf{finite-history tabular agent} uses \cref{eq:tabstate} with $L=4$.
It stores one scalar per state--action pair and applies
\cref{eq:qlearn}. It is statistically efficient when the relevant suffixes are
few and frequently revisited, interpretable because every entry can be
inspected, and incapable of generalization between distinct keys.

The \textbf{reward-only GRU agent} applies \cref{eq:gru} to the complete
interaction sequence. At each decision it concatenates recurrent memory
$z_t$, a learned goal embedding $e_g$, and one-hot goal progress, then feeds
that vector to a multilayer perceptron producing one Q-value per legal action.
The embedding is simply a trainable lookup vector in this baseline; no
geometric loss constrains it.

The \textbf{predictive geometry GRU} shares the same recurrent trunk but has
four coupled outputs: reward value, one-step outcome prediction, positive
cost-to-go, and goal embedding. Parameter sharing asks the memory to retain
features useful across control, prediction, and reachability. It is a
multi-task inductive bias, not a guarantee that a unique latent state will be
identified.

\subsection{Auxiliary objectives and their roles}

Before the current outcome is incorporated, the outcome head predicts
$\widehat P_\theta(o_t|z_t,a_t)$. Its categorical negative log likelihood is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_{\rm pred}=-\log\widehat P_\theta(o_t|z_t,a_t),
\end{equation}
with impossible outcomes masked from the softmax. Predicting after ingesting
$o_t$ would permit trivial copying; the temporal ordering is therefore part of
the scientific validity of the auxiliary task.

For the selected action, reward and cost losses are squared temporal-
difference residuals,
\begin{align}
 \mathcal L_Q&=(Y_t^Q-Q_\theta(z_t,g,j_t,a_t))^2,\\
 \mathcal L_C&=(Y_t^C-C_\theta(z_t,g,j_t,a_t))^2.
 \label{eq:td-losses}
\end{align}
The cost output is transformed to remain positive. The geometry loss is
\cref{eq:geoloss}. The full per-batch objective is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L=\mathcal L_Q+\lambda_P\mathcal L_{\rm pred}
 +\lambda_C\mathcal L_C+\lambda_G\mathcal L_{\rm geom}. \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
The coefficients are necessary because the losses have different numerical
scales. A large prediction weight can improve next-outcome modeling while
diverting capacity from reward; a large geometry weight can make embeddings
faithful to a transient, inaccurate policy. We therefore treat the full model
as a testable architectural proposal rather than presuming that more auxiliary
objectives must improve control.

\subsection{Control performance}

interpreted. An embedding learned by an agent that seldom reaches its goals
mostly describes erroneous action values. We therefore begin with held-out
greedy success and only then ask whether internal quantities have geometric
meaning. Success is averaged equally over goals within a scenario and then
equally over scenarios for the macro-average; this avoids allowing an
environment with more goals to dominate the headline number.

The macro differences are small: the full model exceeds tabular success by

justify a universal superiority claim. They show that adding predictive,
cost, and geometry objectives is compatible with competitive control at this
scale. The scenario-level reversals are scientifically more informative.
Short finite histories are a strong bias in small measurement worlds, where
the same exact suffix recurs often. Recurrent compression becomes more useful
as the qutrit alphabet and possible histories grow, but its larger parameter
space also increases optimization variance.

Conditional intervention counts answer a secondary question: among trials
that finish, how direct are the successful paths? The full agent's value of
4.41 is numerically lowest, but it cannot compensate for a lower success rate
on a particular task. For illustration, a policy that succeeds in one step on
10\% of trials and fails otherwise has conditional duration one, yet is worse
for most purposes than a policy that reliably succeeds in five steps. This is
why \cref{fig:performance} juxtaposes rather than combines the two metrics.

Neural instability is substantial. Mean absolute seed gaps are 2.1 percentage
points for tabular, 8.2 for GRU, and 11.0 for the full model; the largest full
gap is 24.3. Both recurrent agents have zero success on the three-checkpoint
qutrit goal, while tabular reaches 15\%. The plain GRU also has zero success on
the two-step $Z0\_SIC0$ goal.

These failures are consistent with a sparse-credit problem. A long sequence
goal is completed only when several ordered events occur within a short
horizon; early in training, its terminal bonus is rarely observed. The
tabular agent can preserve a lucky exact suffix once encountered, whereas the
recurrent learner must alter a distributed representation without corrupting
other goals. More episodes, curriculum learning, eligibility traces, or
demonstrations could change the ranking. The present comparison evaluates the
specified finite-budget learners, not their asymptotic capacities.

\subsection{Goal-geometry validation}

The embedding loss explicitly encourages policy similarity, so training-loss
fit would be circular evidence. We instead compute pairwise distances from
held-out greedy behavior. Spearman rank correlation is chosen because the
embedding scale is arbitrary and the most defensible first claim is ordinal:
goals represented as nearer should generally require more similar strategies.
If $r_i$ and $s_i$ are the ranks of two lists of pairwise distances, Spearman
correlation is simply their Pearson correlation,
\begin{equation}
 \rho_S=\frac{\sum_i(r_i-\bar r)(s_i-\bar s)}
 {\sqrt{\sum_i(r_i-\bar r)^2\sum_i(s_i-\bar s)^2}}.
 \label{eq:spearman}
\end{equation}
It is invariant under monotone rescaling and therefore does not claim metric
calibration.

Across 12 full-agent runs, embedding distance has mean Spearman correlation
$0.537$ with held-out policy strategy distance (median $0.533$, standard

generalization of the regularized relation. Correlation with more independent
trajectory distance is weaker: mean $0.311$, median $0.274$, standard deviation
$0.303$, range $-0.091$--$0.786$. Geometry is not yet a unique robust object.
The first two PCA axes explain 77.3\% of embedding variance on average.

The gap between policy and trajectory correlations has a useful
interpretation. The regularizer observes local soft policies at sampled
states, so $\rho=0.537$ indicates moderate out-of-sample persistence of its
intended relation. Trajectory signatures additionally incorporate the states
the policy visits, stochastic outcomes, duration, and success. Their weaker
$\rho=0.311$ shows that similar local preferences do not yet imply reliably
similar global behavior. Negative correlations in some seeds establish that
the embedding is not a seed-invariant intrinsic geometry.

Explained PCA variance is reported only to quantify how much of the learned
six-dimensional lookup table can be displayed on a page. A value of 77.3\%
does not establish a two-dimensional manifold: PCA preserves variance rather
than all pairwise distances, the goal set is small, and the embeddings were not
trained to produce spatial coordinates. The inverse-designed study adopts much
stricter dimensional diagnostics.

\subsection{Reachability and intervention displacement}

Reachability evaluation asks a more demanding question than rank-order
similarity: does a learned scalar equal an empirical number of interventions?
We compare the blank-history prediction for each goal with mean hitting time
on successful geometry rollouts. Pearson correlation measures linear ordering
and mean absolute error (MAE)
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{MAE}=\frac1M\sum_{i=1}^M
 |\widehat d_i-\widehat{\E}(\tau_i\mid\tau_i\leq T)|
 \label{eq:mae}
\end{equation}
measures calibration in intervention units. Correlation can be high despite a
wrong scale; low MAE can arise from predicting the mean for a narrow range.
Both are therefore needed.

Across 79 goals with at least one geometry-rollout success, predicted cost
correlates $r=0.471$ with conditional empirical steps and has mean absolute
error 1.75. Predictions are compressed to 2.46--5.89 while observed means span
1.0--10.71. The head contains ordinal information but is not a calibrated
clock. Off-policy minimum backups, function approximation, policy mismatch,
finite-horizon censoring, and conditioning on success all contribute.

The compression of predictions is visible evidence of regression toward
frequently observed targets: very easy and very hard goals are pulled toward a
middle range. Off-policy minimum backups add downward selection bias because a
minimum over noisy action estimates tends to choose an underestimated value.
At the other side, failed trials are absent from the conditional empirical
mean. The comparison is therefore deliberately diagnostic rather than a claim
that either axis is an uncensored ground truth. The success curves in
\cref{fig:curves} preserve the missing reliability information.

\section{Inverse-designing emergent two-dimensional space}
\label{sec:spatial}

\subsection{From a philosophical proposal to an empirical criterion}

The preceding experiments ask whether a finite goal repertoire acquires useful
structure. The stronger hypothesis motivating this project is that ordinary
space itself may be a special form of an agent's goal geometry: goals that are
easy to achieve are near, goals that require many interventions are far, and a
policy is motion through the resulting hodological space. This statement is
not yet an experiment. To make it falsifiable, we need operational definitions
of place, distance, dimension, and trajectory that do not silently provide the
answer to the learner.

Suppose there are $n$ repeatably preparable operational situations
$s_1,\ldots,s_n$ and $n$ corresponding place goals $g_1,\ldots,g_n$. The
ordered policy cost from source $i$ to goal $j$ is
\begin{equation}
 C_{ij}^{\pi}=\E_{\pi_j}[\min(\tau_{g_j},T)\mid s_i],
 \label{eq:restricted-cost}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the evaluation horizon. In a fully successful regime this reduces
to expected hitting time. The reported spatial dissimilarity removes the one
intervention that every goal uses for terminal verification:
\begin{equation}
 D_{ij}=C_{ij}^{\pi}-c_{\rm probe},\qquad c_{\rm probe}=1.
 \label{eq:spatial-cost}
\end{equation}
Subtracting this common overhead is analogous to removing a fixed measurement
latency from every journey. Without it, even $D_{ii}$ would be one although no
movement is needed. The subtraction affects absolute scale but not the learned
policy.

A finite goal system supports an ordinary spatial interpretation only if
several conditions hold together:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \emph{competence}: policies reliably reach every goal from every source;
 \item \emph{localization}: histories contain enough operational information
 to select source-dependent actions, without being handed coordinates;
 \item \emph{metric coherence}: pairwise costs are approximately symmetric and
 obey a common low-distortion distance model;
 \item \emph{dimensional preference}: two dimensions fit much better than one,
 while three add little;
 \item \emph{trajectory coherence}: successive operational observations map to
 local paths in the coordinates derived from all-pairs cost;
 \item \emph{robustness}: these conclusions persist across learned policies,
 random seeds, and controls designed to remove specific ingredients.
\end{enumerate}
No single item is sufficient. A perfect navigator can inhabit a non-Euclidean
metric; a beautiful embedding can summarize failed policies; and a hidden grid
can be invisible to an agent that cannot localize itself.

\subsection{The inverse-design problem}

Let $\theta$ parameterize the initial-state ensemble, quantum instruments, and
observation coarse-graining, and let $\mathcal G$ denote the goal repertoire.
Training algorithm $\mathcal A_{\rm RL}$ maps interaction data to policies
$\pi_{\theta,\mathcal G}$. The outer scientific problem is therefore bilevel:
\begin{align}
 \pi_{\theta,\mathcal G}
 &=\mathcal A_{\rm RL}(\mathcal E_\theta,\mathcal G),\\
 (\theta^\star,\mathcal G^\star)
 &=\argmin_{\theta,\mathcal G}
 J_d\!\left(D_{\pi_{\theta,\mathcal G}}\right).
 \label{eq:bilevel-map}
\end{align}
The inner learner turns the proposed world into behavior; evaluation turns
behavior into a cost matrix; the outer objective judges whether that matrix
has the desired geometry. A useful general objective is
\begin{equation}
 J_d=\lambda_s\operatorname{Stress}_d
 +\lambda_c P_{\rm calibration}+\lambda_n P_{\rm nonmetric}
 +\lambda_a P_{\rm asymmetry}+\lambda_f P_{\rm failure}
 +\lambda_k P_{\rm complexity}.
 \label{eq:bilevel}
\end{equation}
The terms discourage different pathologies. Stress penalizes failure of a
$d$-dimensional Euclidean fit. Calibration compares learned and reproducible
costs. The nonmetric term detects dissimilarities that cannot be exact
Euclidean distances even in higher dimension. Asymmetry detects irreversible
or policy-biased travel. Failure prevents the optimizer from making difficult
pairs disappear behind censoring. Complexity discourages a separate
teleportation action for every goal or an unnecessarily large hidden model.

Optimizing general quantum channels through a stochastic RL loop is expensive
and scientifically opaque as a first experiment. We instead choose a family
whose exact stochastic shortest paths can be computed by a privileged outer
loop, search one physically meaningful instrument parameter, and only then
train coordinate-free agents. This separation makes the construction
auditable: analytic intuition, exact design geometry, and learned empirical
geometry can be compared rather than conflated.

\subsection{Euclidean distance and the dimensional hypothesis}

For candidate coordinates $x_i\in\R^d$, the Euclidean distance is
$\delta_{ij}(X)=\|x_i-x_j\|_2$. Metric multidimensional scaling (MDS) asks for
coordinates whose distances approximate the observed dissimilarities. We use
Kruskal-style normalized raw stress \cite{kruskal1964}:
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Stress}_d(D,X)
 =\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i<j}[\delta_{ij}(X)-D_{ij}]^2}
 {\sum_{i<j}D_{ij}^2}}.
 \label{eq:stress}
\end{equation}
The numerator is total squared distance error; the denominator removes overall
units. Stress zero means exact representation at the requested dimension.
There is no universal threshold at which stress suddenly becomes acceptable:
its interpretation depends on sample size, noise, and comparison conditions.
We therefore emphasize relative gaps among dimensions and matched controls.

The dimensional hypothesis is not merely ``2D stress is small.'' Any finite
metric can often fit better when extra dimensions are added. The characteristic
pattern sought here is
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Stress}_1\gg\operatorname{Stress}_2
 \approx\operatorname{Stress}_3.
 \label{eq:dimension-gap}
\end{equation}
The first inequality rejects a line; the small second improvement says a third
coordinate explains little additional structure. Larger studies should use
held-out points or local dimension estimators to guard against overfitting. On
nine exhaustively evaluated sites, the dimension curve and controls provide a
transparent first test.

MDS is related in spirit to manifold learning, where global coordinates are
reconstructed from local metric relations \cite{tenenbaum2000}. The important
difference is the source of dissimilarity: our $D_{ij}$ is not an externally
measured physical separation. It is the intervention cost of a learned policy.
The resulting axes consequently have no preferred orientation, handedness,
origin, or unit until an external validation frame is applied.

\subsection{Nine-dimensional quantum construction}

\subsubsection{Hilbert space, labels, and source ensemble}

Let
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal H_9=\operatorname{span}\{\lvert x,y\rangle:
 x,y\in\{0,1,2\}\},\qquad
 \rho_0=\lvert1,1\rangle\!\langle1,1\rvert .
 \label{eq:grid-hilbert}
\end{equation}
The nine basis vectors are mutually orthogonal localized states. The canonical
episode starts at the central state $\rho_0$, but all-pairs training uniformly
draws a source from the ensemble
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal S_0=\{|x,y\rangle\!\langle x,y|:x,y\in\{0,1,2\}\}.
 \label{eq:source-ensemble}
\end{equation}
This distinction is important. The central state answers the requested
initial-state specification; random-source episodes teach translation of a
local policy across the entire operational space. A geometry inferred only
from the center would determine one row of $D$, not all pairwise relations.

The coordinate labels $(x,y)$ exist only in environment construction,
exact-cost calculation, and post hoc validation. The learner sees an arbitrary
bijection to symbols A--I. Permuting these symbols before training changes no
available statistical fact. Thus place identity is observable, but adjacency,
axis, handedness, coordinate difference, and Euclidean distance are not.

\subsubsection{Movement actions as quantum instruments}

There are eight movement actions: north, south, east, west, and four
diagonals. Let $s$ abbreviate a basis site and let $f_a(s)$ be its neighboring
destination when action $a$ is legal. Their hidden source-resolved Kraus events
are
\begin{equation}
 K^{(a)}_{s,+}=\sqrt{p_a(s)}\lvert f_a(s)\rangle\!\langle s\rvert,
 \qquad
 K^{(a)}_{s,-}=\sqrt{1-p_a(s)}\lvert s\rangle\!\langle s\rvert .
 \label{eq:move-kraus}
\end{equation}
For a legal axial movement $p_a(s)=1$; for a legal diagonal
$p_a(s)=p_d$; and at an open boundary $p_a(s)=0$ with $f_a(s)$ understood as
$s$. The ``$+$'' branch moves, while the ``$-$'' branch remains in place.

Physical completeness follows explicitly. For each source $s$,
\begin{align}
 K_{s,+}^{(a)\dagger}K_{s,+}^{(a)}
 &=p_a(s)|s\rangle\!\langle s|,\\
 K_{s,-}^{(a)\dagger}K_{s,-}^{(a)}
 &=[1-p_a(s)]|s\rangle\!\langle s|.
\end{align}
Adding branches gives $|s\rangle\!\langle s|$; adding the nine orthogonal
sources gives
\begin{equation}
 \sum_s\sum_{b\in\{+,-\}}
 K_{s,b}^{(a)\dagger}K_{s,b}^{(a)}
 =\sum_s|s\rangle\!\langle s|=I_9.
 \label{eq:grid-completeness}
\end{equation}
Every action is therefore a valid trace-preserving quantum instrument for all
$p_d\in[0,1]$.

These channels also reveal the construction's present boundary. On arbitrary
$\rho=\sum_{s,t}\rho_{st}|s\rangle\!\langle t|$, the source-resolved sum
removes off-diagonal coherences because every term contains the same source
projector on both sides. The movement is a measure-and-prepare,
entanglement-breaking channel on the localized basis: a classical stochastic
walk represented within quantum operations. This is a legitimate quantum
environment and a clean existence proof, but it does not yet use coherent
interference to generate the geometry.

\subsubsection{Observed outcomes as a coarse-graining of hidden events}

The environment need not report the hidden source index or branch. Several
Kraus events can correspond to the same classical outcome, as in
\cref{eq:instrument-map}. In the main world, events are grouped by the place
occupied after the action. The completely positive operation associated with
observing destination $y$ is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I_y^{(a)}(\rho)
 =\sum_{s:f_a(s)=y}K_{s,+}^{(a)}\rho K_{s,+}^{(a)\dagger}
 +K_{y,-}^{(a)}\rho K_{y,-}^{(a)\dagger}.
 \label{eq:grouped-instrument}
\end{equation}
where the first sum includes successful branches arriving at $y$, and the
second term includes a failed attempt originating at $y$. Summing
$\mathcal I_y^{(a)}$ over $y$ recovers the trace-preserving action channel.
Conditioned on observing $y$, the post-measurement state is $|y\rangle\!\langle
y|$ whenever the input is a localized state. The observed symbol is therefore
a place-cell-like localization event.

The blind control uses exactly the same hidden Kraus operators but groups
events into only two observed outcomes: success and failure. It isolates the
effect of observation: hidden connectivity and transition probabilities remain
fixed while location becomes partially observed. Comparing the two asks
whether an underlying spatial graph is enough when the agent cannot tell where
it is.

\subsubsection{A common probe and a homogeneous goal repertoire}

A ninth action is a sharp place probe with projectors
\begin{equation}
 P_s=|s\rangle\!\langle s|,\qquad
 \sum_sP_s^\dagger P_s=I_9.
 \label{eq:place-probe}
\end{equation}
The nine goals all have exactly the same syntax:
\begin{equation}
 g_s=\text{``choose the common probe and observe symbol $s$''.}
 \label{eq:place-goal}
\end{equation}
There is no action called ``go to A,'' no coordinate-conditioned reward, and
no separate detector dedicated to each target. A successful policy must use
movement actions until its operational observation is appropriate and then use
the common probe. Goal names distinguish desired outcomes but do not encode
which actions approach them.

At the beginning of a random-source episode, the environment emits one ordinary
probe result before the task clock starts. This supplies current place identity
without charging a movement or verification cost. It reveals no neighbor
table. From then onward, the agent must learn the transition affordances of
each movement label through interaction. This arrangement cleanly separates
\emph{localization} (which place symbol am I at?) from \emph{geometrization}
(how are place symbols related by action cost?).

\subsubsection{The coordinate-free Markov state}

For the place-observed worlds, the observable decision state is
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde S_t=(g,J_t,\ell_t),
 \label{eq:place-state}
\end{equation}
where $\ell_t$ is the latest arbitrary place symbol. It deliberately discards
the action and older history. This is not a hand-coded coordinate: it contains
no pair $(x,y)$ and no distance. It is a quotient of histories by current
observable affordance. If two histories end with the same $\ell_t$, every
future action has the same outcome distribution, so retaining their different
arrival routes would violate rather than improve Markov abstraction.

Tabular Q-learning over \cref{eq:place-state} must still discover which
movement labels connect which symbols and how their stochastic costs compare.
The blind world cannot use this state because the latest success/failure
outcome does not identify place; it retains the longer finite-history learner.
This matched difference becomes an observability ablation rather than a claim
that memory is never necessary.

\subsection{Why stochastic diagonal motion repairs the metric}

The shortest-path metric induced by four unit-cost cardinal actions is
Manhattan distance,
\begin{equation}
 d_1[(x,y),(x',y')]=|x-x'|+|y-y'|.
 \label{eq:manhattan}
\end{equation}
This recovers the topology and ordering of a square grid but not its ordinary
Euclidean metric
\begin{equation}
 d_2[(x,y),(x',y')]=
 \sqrt{(x-x')^2+(y-y')^2}.
 \label{eq:euclidean-grid}
\end{equation}
In particular, both an axial step and a diagonal step cost one if all eight
moves are deterministic, whereas Euclidean geometry requires the diagonal to
cost $\sqrt2$ times as much.

The instrument supplies a simple operational unit conversion. Repeating a
legal diagonal action with independent success probability $p_d$ gives a
geometric waiting time $N\in\{1,2,\ldots\}$:
\begin{equation}
 \Prob(N=k)=(1-p_d)^{k-1}p_d.
 \end{equation}
Its expectation can be derived from the geometric series,
\begin{align}
 \E N
 &=\sum_{k=1}^\infty k(1-p_d)^{k-1}p_d
 =p_d\frac{d}{dq}\left.\sum_{k=0}^\infty q^k\right|_{q=1-p_d}\\
 &=p_d\frac{1}{[1-(1-p_d)]^2}=\frac1{p_d}.
 \label{eq:geometric-cost}
\end{align}
Matching this expected diagonal cost to $\sqrt2$ predicts
\begin{equation}
 p_d\approx\frac1{\sqrt2}=0.707106\ldots.
 \label{eq:diagonal-prediction}
\end{equation}
This analytic argument is a sanity check, not the fitted result. Open
boundaries and a finite $3\times3$ set mean that a value minimizing global MDS
error need not equal $1/\sqrt2$ exactly.

\subsection{Exact stochastic shortest paths in the outer loop}

For a fixed $p_d$, the privileged design model constructs a directed weighted
graph over the nine localized states. A legal axial edge has expected cost one
and a legal diagonal edge has expected cost $1/p_d$. The optimal exact movement
cost obeys
\begin{equation}
 d^*_{ij}=\min_{a\in\mathcal A_i}
 \left[c(i,a)+d^*_{f_a(i),j}\right],\qquad d^*_{jj}=0,
 \label{eq:grid-shortest-path}
\end{equation}
where $c=1$ or $1/p_d$. This is the deterministic weighted-graph form obtained
after analytically integrating the self-loop failures. Dijkstra's algorithm
computes every source--goal pair because costs are nonnegative.

This exact matrix is used only for economical outer search and as a later
calibration target. The RL agent is not given the matrix, probabilities, graph,
or Dijkstra policy. It samples actual successes and failures and learns from
reward. Separating exact design from learned verification prevents a favorable
analytic construction from being mistaken for evidence that finite-data
behavior recovered it.

\subsection{Classical MDS, Euclidean spectra, and stress refinement}

Classical MDS gives an algebraic test of Euclidean realizability
\cite{torgerson1952,gower1966}. Let $\Delta^{(2)}$ contain squared symmetric
dissimilarities, let
\begin{equation}
 H=I_n-\frac1n\bm1\bm1^\top
 \end{equation}
center vectors by subtracting their mean, and define the double-centered Gram
matrix
\begin{equation}
 B=-\frac12H\Delta^{(2)}H.
 \label{eq:double-centering}
\end{equation}
To see why, suppose centered coordinates form the rows of $X$. Then
$\delta_{ij}^2=x_i^\top x_i+x_j^\top x_j-2x_i^\top x_j$; double centering
cancels the norm terms and leaves $B=XX^\top$. Therefore exact Euclidean
distances produce a positive-semidefinite $B$. If
\begin{equation}
 B=V\diag(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)V^\top,
 \qquad\lambda_1\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_n,
\end{equation}
then the best classical $d$-dimensional coordinates retain the leading
positive eigenvalues:
\begin{equation}
 X_d=V_{1:d}\diag(\sqrt{\lambda_1},\ldots,\sqrt{\lambda_d}).
 \label{eq:cmds-coordinates}
\end{equation}

Classical MDS optimizes an algebraic strain criterion, not exactly the raw
stress in \cref{eq:stress}. We use $X_d$ as a deterministic initialization for
SMACOF (scaling by majorizing a complicated function), an iterative
majorization algorithm that monotonically reduces raw stress. Deterministic
initialization avoids making reported dimension gaps depend on arbitrary plot
seeds.

Negative eigenvalues of $B$ are informative. They cannot be represented as
squared norms in a real Euclidean space, so a large negative spectrum indicates
that the dissimilarity is non-Euclidean even before choosing dimension. We
report
\begin{align}
 V_2^+&=\frac{\lambda_1^++\lambda_2^+}
 {\sum_k\lambda_k^+},\qquad \lambda^+=\max(\lambda,0),
 \label{eq:positive-variance}\\
 P_-&=\frac{\sum_k|\min(\lambda_k,0)|}
 {\sum_k|\lambda_k|}.
 \label{eq:negative-spectrum}
\end{align}
$V_2^+$ asks how much representable Euclidean variation lies in two axes;
$P_-$ asks how much of the centered relation resists any Euclidean
interpretation. They answer different questions and complement stress.

\subsection{Validation metrics and why each is present}

No single scalar certifies emergent space, so the study reports a diagnostic
panel:
\begin{description}[style=nextline]
 \item[All-pairs success.]
 The fraction of 81 ordered source--goal tasks completed by horizon 12. This
 tests whether every entry of the cost matrix describes meaningful navigation.
 \item[Stress in 1D, 2D, and 3D.]
 These values test \cref{eq:dimension-gap} and quantify absolute distance
 distortion at each candidate dimension.
 \item[Positive two-dimensional spectral variance.]
 Equation~\eqref{eq:positive-variance} tests whether two axes dominate the
 Euclidean part of the centered dissimilarity.
 \item[Negative-spectrum fraction.]
 Equation~\eqref{eq:negative-spectrum} detects intrinsically non-Euclidean
 structure that could be hidden by an attractive low-dimensional projection.
 \item[Exact-cost correlation.]
 Pearson correlation between vectorized learned and exact costs tests whether
 RL recovered the intended ordering and approximately linear scale. It is not a
 dimensional test: the Manhattan control can score perfectly.
 \item[Directionality.]
 Before MDS, normalized antisymmetry is retained as
 \begin{equation}
  A(D)=\frac{\|D-D^\top\|_F}{\|D+D^\top\|_F}.
  \label{eq:directionality}
 \end{equation}
 This measures finite-sample, policy, or genuine dynamical irreversibility.
 \item[Procrustes recovery.]
 After learning only, the 2D embedding $X$ is aligned to concealed validation
 coordinates $Y$ by translation, orthogonal transformation, and uniform scale:
 \begin{equation}
  \min_{c>0,R^\top R=I,b}\|cXR+\bm1b^\top-Y\|_F^2.
  \label{eq:procrustes}
 \end{equation}
 If $X_c^\top Y_c=U\Sigma V^\top$, the optimal orthogonal map is
 $R=UV^\top$ \cite{schonemann1966}. We report
 $R_P^2=1-\mathrm{SSE}_{\rm aligned}/\|Y_c\|_F^2$. This tests coordinate
 recovery up to the gauge freedoms of distance geometry.
 \item[Concealed Euclidean distance correlation.]
 This compares pairwise distances without choosing an alignment and provides a
 scale-insensitive check on the intended physical interpretation.
\end{description}

Procrustes coordinates are privileged and never enter policy learning. They
are useful because a correct emergent geometry is identifiable only up to
translation, rotation, reflection, and scale. Yet Procrustes $R_P^2$ cannot
replace stress: Manhattan distances on a square grid can recover the correct
point arrangement after alignment while still distorting metric ratios.

\subsection{One-parameter outer search}

The outer loop evaluates 81 values of $p_d$ evenly spaced from 0.55 to 0.95.
For each value it computes the exact all-pairs matrix, fits 2D coordinates, and
combines 2D stress, negative spectrum, and privileged coordinate-recovery
penalty. The selected value is
\begin{equation}
 p_d^\star=0.715,
 \qquad \frac1{p_d^\star}=1.3986,
 \end{equation}
close to the analytic $1/\sqrt2$ prediction. The analytic value is plotted as a
reference but is not an input target to the numerical objective.

\begin{figure}[p]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.98\textwidth]{results/spatial-hodology/design_optimization.png}
\caption{Inverse design of diagonal success probability (left) and exact MDS
of the selected hitting-cost matrix (right). The red line is $1/\sqrt2$; the
black line is the selected $0.715$. Exact 2D stress is 0.0365. Place labels
carry no coordinate information.}
\label{fig:spatial-design}
\end{figure}

The selected exact matrix has 1D/2D/3D stresses
$0.4305/0.0365/0.0360$, contains 93.4\% of its positive Gram-spectrum variance
in two dimensions, and correlates 0.995 with concealed Euclidean distance. The
cardinal control has 2D stress 0.1416 and negative-spectrum fraction 0.217,
versus 0.081 for the selected instrument. Thus a grid-like arrangement does
not by itself make the operational metric Euclidean.

These exact numbers establish that the designed task is suitable for the inner
learning experiment. One dimension fails badly: nine points on a line cannot
simultaneously preserve the corner, edge, and center relations. Moving from
two to three dimensions changes stress by only $0.0005$, so the small remaining
error is not substantially repaired by an extra spatial axis. The 0.995
distance correlation says the cost ordering and relative scale closely match
the concealed Euclidean lattice, while the nonzero negative spectrum records
the unavoidable residual of a small weighted graph rather than a continuous
plane. The claim is approximate Euclidean compatibility, not exact isometry.

\subsection{Inner learning problem and policy extraction}

For the place-observed worlds, the learner is the standard tabular Q-learning
algorithm of \cref{eq:qlearn} applied to state \cref{eq:place-state}, with
$\alpha=0.1$, $\gamma=0.95$, and epsilon-greedy exploration decaying from 1.0
to 0.05. Source and target are sampled independently and uniformly for each of
6,000 episodes. Uniform coverage gives every ordered relation a chance to
affect the Q table; it also includes source-equals-goal episodes, which teach
the common verification action.

The learned reward policy is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\pi_g(a|\ell)=
 \mathbb 1\!\left[a\in\argmax_{a'}Q(g,J,\ell,a')\right]
 \label{eq:spatial-greedy-policy}
\end{equation}
with deterministic tie-breaking at evaluation. Notice what is and is not
learned. The table learns action values and thereby local navigation. MDS does
not provide input to the policy and the Q table has no $x$ or $y$ columns.
Conversely, the final spatial coordinates are computed from empirical policy
costs, not read from the table or hidden basis labels. Control learning and
geometric reconstruction are two distinct stages.

For each ordered pair $(i,j)$, evaluation resets to source $i$, executes
$\widehat\pi_{g_j}$ without exploration, and repeats 100 times. Let
$\widehat s_{ij}$ be the success fraction and let
$\widetilde\tau_{ij}^{(m)}=\min(\tau_{ij}^{(m)},T)$ assign the horizon to a
failure. The empirical restricted cost is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat C_{ij}=\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^M\widetilde\tau_{ij}^{(m)},
 \qquad M=100,quad T=12.
 \label{eq:empirical-spatial-cost}
\end{equation}
The common final-probe cost is then subtracted as in
\cref{eq:spatial-cost}. Using the restricted rather than success-conditional
mean ensures failure increases distance instead of disappearing from it.

MDS requires a symmetric dissimilarity, whereas finite policy estimates are
ordered. We therefore form
\begin{equation}
 \widehat D^{\rm sym}_{ij}
 =\frac12(\widehat D_{ij}+\widehat D_{ji})
 \label{eq:symmetrization}
\end{equation}
for the Euclidean hypothesis and separately report
\cref{eq:directionality}. Symmetrization is a declared projection onto the
class of ordinary metric candidates, not evidence that the original process
was reversible.

\subsection{Matched controls and experimental scale}

The three worlds differ one ingredient at a time:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{optimized place-observed}: four axial and four cost-matched
 diagonal actions; a movement reports its destination symbol;
 \item \textbf{optimized blind}: identical hidden movement Kraus maps and
 $p_d$, but a movement reports only success or failure;
 \item \textbf{cardinal place-observed}: destination symbols remain available,
 but diagonal actions are removed, leaving the Manhattan metric.
\end{enumerate}
The blind comparison targets observability and state estimation. The cardinal
comparison targets metric isotropy while holding localization and basic
competence favorable. Together they distinguish ``there is a grid,'' ``the
agent can navigate,'' and ``the learned cost is Euclidean-like.''

Each of three worlds and three seeds receives 6,000 uniformly sampled
source--goal episodes. Evaluation uses 100 trials for all 81 ordered pairs:
54,000 training and 72,900 evaluation episodes in total. Failures contribute a
finite restricted-mean penalty at horizon 12. The learned matrix is
symmetrized for MDS, while normalized antisymmetry is retained separately.

The resulting total is 54,000 training episodes and 72,900 evaluation episodes.
Three seeds are still too few for narrow confidence intervals, but exhaustive
all-pairs evaluation within each run is much stronger than evaluating only the
canonical center reset. Every aggregate in \cref{tab:spatial-results} is the
mean and sample standard deviation of three independently trained policies.

\subsection{Results: competence before geometry}

The proposed place-observed learner achieves all 81 ordered tasks in every
seed. This result removes the most serious ambiguity in a hitting-cost matrix:
none of its entries is dominated by the failure penalty. The cardinal control
also reaches 100\%, proving that navigation success alone does not select the
proposed metric. By contrast, the blind learner succeeds on only
$0.482\pm0.023$ of ordered trials. Its poor geometric fit must therefore be
read partly as a control failure and not purely as a change of intrinsic
dimension.

Why does observation matter so much? A destination symbol makes
\cref{eq:place-state} Markov: after every move the same symbol implies the same
future transition law, independent of arrival route. Success/failure alone
does not. The blind learner must infer place from a bounded action/outcome
history, and different hidden sites can produce identical recent binary
records. A policy conditioned on such an aliased state must compromise between
incompatible actions. This degrades success, inflates restricted costs, and
introduces direction-dependent sampling error.

\subsection{Results: selecting two dimensions}

The proposed construction has a decisive dimension gap: learned stress falls
from $0.372$ in 1D to $0.071$ in 2D, while a third coordinate improves it only
to $0.064$. Procrustes $R^2=0.975$ confirms recovery of the concealed
arrangement up to rotation, reflection, translation, and scale. Coordinate
recovery alone would be misleading: the cardinal control has $R_P^2\simeq1$
but twice the 2D stress because its metric is Manhattan.

The first stress drop is the main dimensional evidence. In relative terms,
two dimensions remove about 81\% of the one-dimensional normalized stress,
whereas a third dimension removes only about 10\% of the remaining stress.
The residual $0.071$ is expected to exceed the exact value $0.0365$: the learned
policy sometimes selects a longer route, diagonal travel is sampled rather
than analytically averaged, and each ordered pair has finite evaluation data.
The small standard deviation across seeds indicates that the dimension pattern
is more stable than the orientation of any individual embedding.

Procrustes $R_P^2=0.975$ means that after removing geometrically irrelevant
translation, orthogonal frame, and scale, only about 2.5\% of concealed
coordinate sum-of-squares remains as alignment error. This is strong recovery
of point arrangement. The cardinal control's perfect value is the decisive
caution: an embedding can put corners, edges, and center in their expected
visual locations while systematically misrepresenting how far apart they are.
Stress, not visual recognizability, detects that difference.

The learned/exact cost correlation $0.936\pm0.003$ asks whether RL recovered
the intended stochastic shortest-path problem. It is high but not perfect,
consistent with a near-optimal policy and finite geometric waiting times. The
cardinal control reaches correlation one because its deterministic tabular
problem is learned exactly. Yet its stress remains $0.142$. Calibration to a
non-Euclidean target cannot make the target Euclidean; this is why exact-cost
correlation and metric fit belong in separate columns.

Directionality is $0.122\pm0.037$ in the proposed world. The underlying exact
cost is symmetric, so the learned asymmetry is not interpreted as physical
time orientation. It reflects independent finite samples, stochastic
diagonals, tie-breaking among near-equivalent routes, and imperfect policies.
The deterministic cardinal control has zero directionality. The blind value
is larger because state aliasing makes policy errors depend strongly on source
and target. Reporting this discarded component makes the symmetry assumption
auditable.

\subsection{Trajectories as motion in the reconstructed coordinates}

Having inferred $X$ from the complete symmetrized cost matrix, we can map a
new policy rollout into that coordinate system. If its observed place sequence
is $(\ell_0,\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_m)$, the plotted path is

 \label{eq:embedded-trajectory}
\end{equation}
This is not a latent-state decoder trained on coordinates. It is a lookup from
operational place symbols to MDS coordinates derived solely from learned
all-pairs difficulty. Movement failures appear as repeated points; successful
local actions connect nearby reconstructed places; the final probe verifies
the target without adding movement distance.

\subsection{Failed constructions and the corrections they motivated}
\label{sec:spatial-failures}

Negative stages were retained because each rules out an initially tempting but
insufficient definition of emergent space.

\paragraph{A single origin produces routes, not a space.}
The first learner always reset at the central state. It achieved roughly
$0.92$--$0.95$ success on that reset distribution, which at first looked
encouraging. When evaluated from arbitrary sources, success fell to about
$0.25$. The policy had learned a star of source-specific routes from the center
and had too little evidence for local behavior elsewhere. More fundamentally,
one source determines only $D_{\mathrm{center},j}$. A 2D geometry requires
relations among all places. Uniform source--goal training corrected both the
coverage defect and the conceptual definition.

\paragraph{A hidden spatial graph is not automatically an agent's space.}
With uniform sources but binary success/failure movement observations, the
learner reached only $0.482$ all-pairs success. The same Kraus connectivity that
supports the positive result was present, yet it was not operationally
available through the learner's finite memory. This failure separates a space
in the simulator designer's representation from a hodological space usable by
the agent. It motivates recurrent predictive localization as the next, more
ambitious experiment.

\paragraph{More remembered detail can make geometry worse.}
When destination symbols were introduced but the agent retained a literal
six-event history, navigation success rose to approximately $0.998$. Its 2D
stress nevertheless remained near $0.16$. The state table assigned distinct
keys to the same current place depending on arrival route. Those keys received
unequal data and learned slightly different policies and costs, fragmenting one
operational place into many representational states. The failure illustrates
that memory capacity and state quality are not synonymous.

\paragraph{Quotienting by future affordance repairs the representation.}
The final PlaceQAgent keeps only $(g,J,\ell)$. Histories ending at the same
place symbol are pooled because they imply the same next-outcome law for every
action. This predictive equivalence increases sample sharing, reaches 100\%
all-pairs success, and lowers mean 2D stress to 0.071. The correction is not
coordinate supervision; it is the minimal Markov quotient supported by the
observation interface.

Together these stages identify three conditions for the present spatial
result: broad all-pairs experience, enough observation or memory for
operational localization, and a representation that merges histories with the
same future affordances. The tuned diagonal cost is a fourth condition for
Euclidean rather than merely grid-topological structure.

\subsection{Exactly what this construction establishes}

The positive conclusion can now be stated without either underselling or
overclaiming it. There exists a valid nine-dimensional quantum instrument
family, a common-probe goal repertoire, and a coordinate-free RL state for
which independently learned all-pairs intervention cost strongly prefers two
dimensions, closely recovers a concealed square arrangement, and maps learned
action histories to stochastic local paths. Matched controls show that
competence, localization, arrangement recovery, and Euclidean metric quality
are distinct.

The result is an existence proof in a deliberately classical corner of quantum
theory. Localized basis states, a sharp place outcome, open boundaries, and
measure-and-prepare motion were designed. The agent reconstructs their metric
relations but does not discover an unanticipated number of places from raw
coherent dynamics. A stronger emergence claim will require aliased or weak
observations, learned recurrent localization, larger and topologically varied
systems, coherent channels, held-out landmarks, and geometry that remains
stable when no privileged place basis is built into the simulator.

\section{A predictive spatial atlas without online place symbols}
\label{sec:predictive-atlas}

\subsection{The information-boundary problem}

The existence construction in \cref{sec:spatial} gives its controller a sharp
place symbol before each decision. This is weaker than providing a coordinate:
the labels $A,\ldots,I$ carry no adjacency or metric semantics. Nevertheless,
the observation already answers the question ``where am I?'' in a discrete
operational sense. If space is to be constructed from an agent's own
goal-directed experience, localization should itself be learned.

We therefore retain the same hidden movement geometry and place goals while
changing the observation interface. Movement reports only success or failure.
Four weak binary probes provide overlapping evidence about place, and a sharp
landmark probe is available only as a terminal commitment. The agent must turn
the weak history into a predictive belief, propagate that belief through blind
motion, and decide when it is ready to claim the goal. This creates a finite
partially observable stochastic-shortest-path problem
\cite{kaelbling1998}. It also makes the word \emph{place} operational: place is
represented by predictions of possible future landmark experiences rather
than by a current simulator label.

The online information boundary is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I_t=\{g,(a_1,o_1),\ldots,(a_t,o_t)\}.
 \label{eq:atlas-information}
\end{equation}
The controller is not given $\rho_t$, any Kraus operator, response
probabilities, lattice coordinates, or the exact landmark that the terminal
probe would report. Source preparation and hidden-state decoding are used only
to construct balanced experiments and audit results. The terminal report can
label a completed calibration scan or score a navigation commitment, but it
cannot inform a later action in that episode.

\subsection{Weak quantum beacons}

Let the concealed site basis again be
$\{|s\rangle:s=0,\ldots,8\}$. Beacon action $b\in\{1,\ldots,4\}$ is a binary
quantum instrument with diagonal Kraus operators
\begin{align}
 B^{(b)}_0&=\sum_{s=0}^{8}\sqrt{1-q_{bs}}\,
 |s\rangle\langle s|,\\
 B^{(b)}_1&=\sum_{s=0}^{8}\sqrt{q_{bs}}\,
 |s\rangle\langle s|.
 \label{eq:beacon-kraus}
\end{align}
The validity calculation is immediate but worth stating:
\begin{equation}
 B^{(b)\dagger}_0B^{(b)}_0+B^{(b)\dagger}_1B^{(b)}_1
 =\sum_s[(1-q_{bs})+q_{bs}]|s\rangle\langle s|=I.
 \label{eq:beacon-complete}
\end{equation}
For a localized input $\rho_s=|s\rangle\langle s|$, the probability of report
one is
\begin{equation}
 \Prob(O=1\mid s,b)=\Tr(B^{(b)}_1\rho_sB^{(b)\dagger}_1)=q_{bs},
\end{equation}
and the normalized posterior quantum state remains $\rho_s$ for either report.
The beacon is consequently quantum nondemolition (QND) on the localized
states: it acquires noisy information without moving the system.

The four fields vary horizontally, vertically, diagonally, and
anti-diagonally. Their response probabilities lie between 0.05 and 0.95. No
single binary report identifies one of nine alternatives; even one complete
four-beacon cycle is strongly ambiguous. Yet the response vectors
\begin{equation}
 q_s=(q_{1s},q_{2s},q_{3s},q_{4s})
\end{equation}
are distinct. Repetition accumulates statistical evidence while leaving the
localized state fixed. This is the simplest controlled realization of a weak
landmark field.

For comparison, the null environment sets $q_{bs}=1/2$ for every $b,s$. Its
scan has identical length, alphabet, model capacity, optimization, and delayed
labels, but
\begin{equation}
 \Prob(h\mid s)=\Prob(h)
\end{equation}
for every scan history $h$. No classifier can exceed the uniform-prior Bayes
accuracy $1/9$ in expectation.

\subsection{Delayed landmark prediction as predictive-state learning}

A scan applies the action sequence $(1,2,3,4)^{12}$ and records the 48
action/outcome pairs
\begin{equation}
 h=((b_1,o_1),\ldots,(b_{48},o_{48})).
\end{equation}
Only after the scan does the common sharp instrument
\begin{equation}
 L_s=|s\rangle\langle s|,
 \qquad s=0,\ldots,8,
 \label{eq:atlas-landmark}
\end{equation}
provide the target. The desired output is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat b_0(s\mid h)\approx
 \Prob(\text{terminal landmark }s\mid h).
 \label{eq:atlas-posterior}
\end{equation}
This is not density-matrix regression. It predicts an intervention/outcome
counterfactual that the agent can operationally test. In the language of
predictive-state representations, histories are equivalent insofar as they
support the same future-test predictions \cite{littman2001}.

Each observed pair is converted to a learned token vector $x_t$. A GRU updates
its hidden memory according to
\begin{align}
 r_t&=\sigma(W_rx_t+U_rh_{t-1}+b_r),\\
 u_t&=\sigma(W_ux_t+U_uh_{t-1}+b_u),\\
 \widetilde h_t&=\tanh[W_hx_t+U_h(r_t\odot h_{t-1})+b_h],\\
 h_t&=(1-u_t)\odot h_{t-1}+u_t\odot\widetilde h_t.
 \label{eq:atlas-gru}
\end{align}
The reset gate $r_t$ controls how much previous memory enters the candidate;
the update gate $u_t$ interpolates between old and proposed memory. A
softmax classifier maps the final state to nine probabilities,
\begin{equation}
 \widehat b_0(s\mid h)=
 \frac{\exp(w_s^\top h_{48}+c_s)}
 {\sum_j\exp(w_j^\top h_{48}+c_j)}.
 \label{eq:atlas-softmax}
\end{equation}
Minimizing negative log likelihood,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_{\rm loc}=-\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N
 \log \widehat b_0(s_i\mid h_i),
 \label{eq:atlas-nll}
\end{equation}
is proper: in the population limit it is uniquely minimized by the true
conditional distribution. Accuracy tests the maximum-probability decision,
whereas NLL strongly penalizes confident mistakes. We also report the
multiclass Brier score
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Brier}=\frac1N\sum_i\sum_s
 [\widehat b_0(s\mid h_i)-\mathbf1\{s_i=s\}]^2,
 \label{eq:atlas-brier}
\end{equation}
posterior confidence, and entropy. These metrics distinguish useful
uncertainty estimates from merely correct class labels.

Because the simulator's $q_{bs}$ are known to the offline analyst, an exact
Bayes ceiling can be computed by multiplying Bernoulli likelihoods. It is not
used for training. Agreement between the learned and Bayes accuracies tells us
whether residual error comes from insufficient observations or failed
optimization.

\subsection{Learning a movement model from landmark-anchored surveys}

Localization alone is not navigation. The agent must know how its
interventions transform the predictive place distribution. For each previously
verified landmark $s$ and blind movement $a$, a survey records the binary
movement outcome $o$ and then terminates with landmark $s'$. Repeated trials
estimate the joint controlled kernel
\begin{equation}
 \widehat T_a(o,s'\mid s)
 =\widehat\Prob(O_t=o,S_{t+1}=s'\mid S_t=s,A_t=a).
 \label{eq:atlas-joint-transition}
\end{equation}
The destination probe is delayed until after the move, so coordinates and
Kraus events are unnecessary. However, the verified source and destination
landmarks are supervision. This phase is best understood as empirical chart
calibration, not autonomous discovery of the chart's labels.

Marginalizing the report yields the planning transition model,
\begin{equation}
 \widehat P_a(s'\mid s)=\sum_o\widehat T_a(o,s'\mid s).
 \label{eq:atlas-transition}
\end{equation}
Retaining the joint tensor remains essential at execution time. Given belief
$b_t(s)$, selected movement $a_t$, and observed report $o_t$, Bayes filtering
gives
\begin{equation}
 b_{t+1}(s')=
 \frac{\sum_s b_t(s)\widehat T_{a_t}(o_t,s'\mid s)}
 {\sum_{j,s}b_t(s)\widehat T_{a_t}(o_t,j\mid s)}.
 \label{eq:atlas-filter}
\end{equation}
Thus a success report and a failure report generally produce different
predictive positions even though neither names a destination. The mean
total-variation discrepancy between the empirical and privileged exact
kernels is only $0.0144\pm0.0008$, indicating that transition-estimation error
is small in the production regime.

\subsection{Belief-state stochastic-shortest-path planning}

For every goal landmark $g$, value iteration solves
\begin{align}
 V_g(g)&=0,\\
 V_g(s)&=\min_a\left[1+\sum_{s'}
 \widehat P_a(s'\mid s)V_g(s')\right],\qquad s\ne g,
 \label{eq:atlas-bellman}
\end{align}
with action cost
\begin{equation}
 Q_g(s,a)=1+\sum_{s'}\widehat P_a(s'\mid s)V_g(s').
 \label{eq:atlas-qcost}
\end{equation}
These are stochastic-shortest-path rather than discounted-reward equations.
One unit is charged for every movement; the absorbing goal has zero future
movement cost. The online action rule averages over uncertainty,
\begin{equation}
 a_t^*=\argmin_a\sum_s b_t(s)Q_g(s,a).
 \label{eq:atlas-belief-action}
\end{equation}
When the posterior mode equals $g$, the controller invokes the terminal probe.
A correct outcome succeeds and an incorrect outcome fails immediately.

Equation~\eqref{eq:atlas-belief-action} is a tractable approximation to exact
belief-MDP planning. Exact planning would assign a value directly to every
continuous belief and account for the future informational value of movement
outcomes. Here state-level costs are computed first and linearly averaged. The
approximation is appropriate when initial beliefs are sharp and dynamics are
nearly deterministic, but it is not generally optimal. Its empirical success
is therefore evaluated rather than assumed.

The controller performs no further beacon scans after its initial scan. New
uncertainty enters only through blind movements and is propagated by
\cref{eq:atlas-filter}. This makes saved paths sequences of predictive beliefs,
not privileged state labels.

\subsection{Experimental design and matched controls}

The production study uses three fixed seeds. For each neural condition and
seed, 400 calibration and 200 held-out scans are generated per landmark and
the GRU is trained for 35 epochs. Each scan contains twelve cycles. Movement
surveys use 100 trials for every one of nine sources and eight actions.
Frozen navigation uses 100 episodes for each of 81 ordered source--goal pairs
with at most twelve movements.

The resulting scale is 21,600 transition-survey trials and 97,200 navigation
episodes. Navigation alone contains 4,665,600 weak-beacon outcomes. Nine model
checkpoints, all per-seed confusion/cost/success/transition matrices, and
complete trajectory audits are retained.

Four navigation conditions isolate distinct questions:
\begin{description}[style=nextline]
 \item[Online oracle] Starts from a one-hot exact-place belief but uses the same
 learned transition model and planner. It estimates the loss due specifically
 to predictive localization.
 \item[Full history] Uses all 48 weak outcomes. This is the proposed atlas.
 \item[Last cycle] Collects the same 48 outcomes but discards the first 44
 before inference. Architecture and sensor cost are matched while usable
 temporal evidence is ablated.
 \item[Null fields] Uses all 48 outcomes from place-independent beacons. This
 tests whether recurrence, labels, or grid symmetry can produce the result in
 the absence of localization information.
\end{description}

The movement-cost matrix excludes the fixed 48-probe scan and one terminal
commitment because those add common overhead to every pair and would compress
relative spatial variation. Total interventions are nevertheless reported:
the full-history agent uses approximately 50.5 interventions per episode while
its mean movement cost is 1.74. Removing common overhead from geometry is not a
claim that sensing is free; it exposes a major efficiency limitation.

\subsection{The failed pilot and why it matters}

The first pilot used eight scan cycles, 200 calibration examples per landmark,
and 20 epochs. It obtained 86.5\% localization and 88.7\% navigation, but 2D
stress remained 0.184. Moderate competence did not preserve the metric. This
failure motivated a longer scan and independently confirms that success and
geometric fidelity are different tests.

A second pilot used twelve cycles, 300 examples per landmark, and 30 epochs. It
reached 97.1\% localization, 97.7\% navigation, 2D stress 0.079, and Procrustes
$R^2=0.984$. Only then was the three-seed production protocol frozen, with 400
calibration examples and 35 epochs. Pilot outputs are not mixed into the
production aggregate; their diagnostic values are retained in the project
log.

\subsection{What the predictive atlas establishes}

The predictive atlas strengthens the earlier existence proof. There exists a
valid weak-instrument interface for which an agent can learn an operational
place posterior from temporal evidence, propagate it through blind stochastic
actions, achieve arbitrary landmark goals, and recover an approximately
two-dimensional Euclidean goal-cost geometry without receiving an exact online
place report. The memory and null controls identify accumulated informative
evidence as a necessary causal ingredient.

Several primitives remain designed. The simulator contains nine localized
basis states; the terminal outcome supplies supervised landmark identity;
transition surveys begin from verified landmarks; beacon instruments commute
with the place basis; and movement is entanglement breaking. Hence the agent
learns to localize within a supplied landmark vocabulary rather than
discovering an unforeseen decomposition of Hilbert space. The fixed scan also
costs 48 interventions, far more than the typical journey. The result should be
read as the first learned predictive atlas, not as generic emergence of
physical space from arbitrary quantum dynamics.

\section{Active atlas construction and an epistemic fiber}
\label{sec:active-atlas}

\subsection{Why sensing must enter the control problem}

The predictive atlas in \cref{sec:predictive-atlas} always performs 48 weak
probes before moving. This isolates the role of recurrent evidence, but the
sensor budget is imposed by the experimenter rather than chosen by the agent.
If place is an operational resource for goal pursuit, the agent should acquire
only the distinctions whose expected benefit justifies their cost.

We now give every intervention the same unit price,
\begin{equation}
 c_{\rm sense}=c_{\rm move}=c_{\rm commit}=1.
 \label{eq:active-unit-cost}
\end{equation}
The agent begins from a uniform landmark belief, selects weak beacons or blind
movements, and eventually invokes the terminal landmark probe. A false
commitment ends the episode. The primary question is whether adaptive
observation can preserve a common two-dimensional movement geometry while
using substantially fewer interventions than fixed scanning.

This is a value-of-information problem inside a POMDP, not a preprocessing
problem. An observation is useful when its possible outcomes alter a valuable
future decision. Its value depends on the current posterior, goal, action
model, remaining options, and failure cost.

\subsection{Boundary homing: a successful policy that destroys geometry}

The first active pilot retained the open-boundary translations of
\cref{sec:predictive-atlas}. It produced a decisive negative result. Repeating
north drives all possible vertical coordinates to the top boundary; repeating
west then drives them to the left boundary. A controller can therefore
\emph{synchronize} an unknown ensemble at a corner. In one pilot the null-
sensor condition reached every corner without sensing.

No hidden coordinate leaked to the controller. Boundary homing was rational
goal-directed behavior. Yet it erased the source distinctions from which an
all-pairs geometry must be inferred. Near-identical open-loop routines from
every source cannot support source-sensitive distance even when terminal
success is perfect. The episode illustrates why competence is necessary but
not sufficient for spatial emergence.

The correction was structural rather than a larger uncertainty penalty. We
require every blind movement kernel $P_a$ to be doubly stochastic,
\begin{equation}
 P_a\mathbf1=\mathbf1,
 \qquad
 \mathbf1^\top P_a=\mathbf1^\top.
 \label{eq:doubly-stochastic}
\end{equation}
Consequently the uniform prior is invariant,
\begin{equation}
 b_{\rm unif}P_a=b_{\rm unif},
 \label{eq:uniform-invariant}
\end{equation}
and blind movement cannot manufacture localization. The null-sensor production
condition then returns to chance.

\subsection{Coherent random-unitary movement}

On the concealed $3\times3$ arrangement, north exchanges the top and middle
rows while leaving the bottom row fixed; south exchanges the middle and bottom
rows. East and west similarly exchange adjacent column layers. The resulting
permutation operators are local involutions,
\begin{equation}
 U_a^2=I,
 \qquad U_a^\dagger U_a=I.
 \label{eq:layer-involution}
\end{equation}
The diagonal operators compose one horizontal and one vertical layer swap.
These actions are contextual edge-layer exchanges rather than ordinary global
translations, but together they generate the full lattice connectivity.

Movement is implemented by the two-outcome random-unitary instrument
\begin{equation}
 M^{(a)}_0=\sqrt{p_a}U_a,
 \qquad
 M^{(a)}_1=\sqrt{1-p_a}I,
 \label{eq:random-unitary-move}
\end{equation}
where axial actions use $p_a=1$ and diagonals use $p_a=0.715$. Its completeness
is
\begin{equation}
 M_0^{(a)\dagger}M_0^{(a)}+M_1^{(a)\dagger}M_1^{(a)}
 =p_aI+(1-p_a)I=I.
\end{equation}
Conditional on the reported outcome, a coherent superposition evolves by
$U_a$ or $I$. Thus movement no longer measures and reprepares a hidden source
site. The beacons are still diagonal QND instruments, but this is the first
spatial experiment here whose movement preserves quantum coherence.

The generated classical graph retains the earlier exact stochastic-shortest-
path matrix: adjacent axial layer crossings cost one and diagonal crossings
cost $1/0.715\simeq\sqrt2$. Exact 2D stress remains 0.0365 and privileged
Procrustes recovery is one. Reversibility removes the homing confound without
sacrificing the designed spatial target.

\subsection{Learning observation and transition tables}

The active controller must imagine the consequences of candidate probes. For
every verified landmark $s$ and beacon $q$, 200 delayed-label trials estimate
\begin{equation}
 \widehat O_q(o\mid s)=\widehat\Prob(O=o\mid S=s,Q=q),
 \label{eq:learned-beacon-table}
\end{equation}
with a symmetric one-half pseudocount. Given outcome $o$, Bayesian filtering is
\begin{equation}
 b_{q,o}(s)=
 \frac{b(s)\widehat O_q(o\mid s)}
 {\sum_jb(j)\widehat O_q(o\mid j)}.
 \label{eq:active-beacon-update}
\end{equation}
Mean absolute response-table error is $0.0233\pm0.0057$ across seeds.

Landmark-anchored movement surveys likewise estimate
$\widehat T_a(o,s'\mid s)$, and \cref{eq:atlas-filter} updates beliefs after
motion. Movement-model total-variation error is $0.0220\pm0.0026$. Value
iteration on its outcome-marginal kernel supplies $V_g(s)$ and $Q_g(s,a)$.
Exact fields and kernels enter only named oracle/audit conditions.

This is self-calibration relative to an operational landmark vocabulary, not
unsupervised discovery of that vocabulary. Sharp terminal labels still anchor
both empirical tables.

\subsection{Goal-relative policy partitions}

We compare two meanings of useful information. The first asks only which next
decision is correct for the present goal. Define
\begin{equation}
 d_g(s)=
 \begin{cases}
  \mathrm{commit},&s=g,\\
  \argmin_aQ_g(s,a),&s\ne g.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:policy-partition}
\end{equation}
A place posterior induces decision-class mass
\begin{equation}
 m_g(d\mid b)=\sum_{s:d_g(s)=d}b(s),
\end{equation}
and next-decision Bayes risk
\begin{equation}
 r_g(b)=1-\max_dm_g(d\mid b).
 \label{eq:decision-risk}
\end{equation}
Places that require the same next movement are deliberately quotiented. This is
the pragmatic state of \cref{sec:rl}: it preserves distinctions useful now,
not necessarily every distinction required by every future goal.

With decision-error penalty $\lambda=100$, finite sensing depth $D=3$ is
evaluated exactly:
\begin{align}
 W_0(b,g)&=\lambda r_g(b),\\
 W_d(b,g)&=\min\left\{\lambda r_g(b),
 \min_{q\in\mathcal B}\left[
 1+\sum_o\Prob(o\mid b,q)W_{d-1}(b_{q,o},g)
 \right]\right\}.
 \label{eq:active-voi}
\end{align}
If the second branch is cheaper, the minimizing unit-cost beacon is sampled;
otherwise the modal movement or commitment is executed. The tree is replanned
after every outcome, and values are cached by goal and rounded posterior.

Depth one failed in pilot testing. One weak result often leaves the modal
decision unchanged, assigning it zero myopic classification value even when a
short sequence would cross a decision boundary. Depth three recognizes that
temporally extended value.

\subsection{Atlas-preserving active sensing}

The second controller treats the full landmark distribution as a representation
to preserve across all goals. It selects the beacon with maximum expected
entropy reduction
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{EIG}(b,q)=H(b)-
 \sum_o\Prob(o\mid b,q)H(b_{q,o})
 \label{eq:active-eig}
\end{equation}
until $max_sb(s)\ge0.95$. It then follows belief-weighted $Q_g$ and senses
again only if uncertainty violates this reliability constraint.

This policy solves a constrained rather than penalty-only problem: minimize
unit-cost sensing subject to landmark confidence. It is less aggressively goal
relative, but it maintains one chart usable by every goal. Comparing it with
\cref{eq:active-voi} asks whether locally sufficient pragmatic states assemble
into a common geometry.

\subsection{Conditions and production scale}

Six conditions separate sensing cost, calibration, representation, and hidden
dynamics:
\begin{description}[style=nextline]
 \item[Oracle] Exact online one-hot landmark; no sensing.
 \item[Goal-relative active] Learned fields and the depth-three policy
 partition rule.
 \item[Exact-sensor active] Same controller with privileged exact beacon fields;
 this is an information-model upper bound, not an online agent claim.
 \item[Fixed-12] Twelve cycles, or 48 learned-model beacon updates, before
 movement.
 \item[Atlas-preserving active] Expected-information-gain sensing to the 0.95
 reliability constraint.
 \item[Active null] Same active computation with learned place-independent
 fair-coin fields.
\end{description}

Production uses three seeds, 200 beacon trials per site/action, 100 movement
trials per source/action, and 50 navigation trials for every ordered pair. The
six conditions give 72,900 held-out navigation episodes. A 60-intervention
safety deadline censors failures in the restricted total-cost analysis.
Movement failures analogously receive the 12-move horizon cost. By contrast,
the sensing matrix records beacon actions actually taken, even on failed
episodes: it is a resource decomposition, and replacing failed trials by the
deadline would make a competence penalty masquerade as sensor use.

\subsection{What active construction establishes}

There exists a valid coherent movement family and learned weak-sensor model for
which an agent can rationally reduce observation by more than 60\%, retain high
all-pairs competence, and preserve a strong two-dimensional movement atlas.
Matched controls show why it works: reversible motion prevents blind
synchronization, learned place-dependent beacons provide the necessary
information, and a representation preserving the full landmark posterior
maintains global metric coherence.

The result also identifies a fundamental trade-off. Pragmatic compression can
be optimal for an immediate goal yet incompatible with a single stable space.
The emergence of a common atlas is therefore an informational achievement, not
an automatic consequence of competent action.

Landmark anchors, tabular observation/transition models, designed layer-swap
actions, diagonal QND sensing, a hand-set three-step horizon and penalty, and a
small grid remain. Movement preserves coherence, but the agent neither exploits
phase nor discovers a place basis from coherent data. The epistemic fiber is a
research representation, not yet geometry with bundle structure.

\section{Exactly soluble hodology in a qubit and a qutrit}
\label{sec:lowdim}

\subsection{The dimensional question and the hidden ambiguity}

The preceding spatial environments use nine orthogonal vectors as nine
landmarks. This is transparent and experimentally useful, but it risks
suggesting a false principle: that nine goals require a nine-dimensional
Hilbert space. A Hilbert-space dimension limits the number of states that are
perfectly distinguishable in one shot; it does not limit the number of
distinct rays, controlled histories, measurement effects, or goal languages.
A qubit has only two orthogonal basis vectors but continuously many pure
states. The correct question is therefore not ``how many goals fit in
$\mathbb C^d$?'' but ``which part of the complete controlled process carries
their geometry?''

Three state spaces must be kept separate:
\begin{description}[style=nextline]
 \item[Physical quantum state] The density operator $\rho$ changed by the
 chosen instruments. Its dimension is fixed by the Hilbert space.
 \item[Predictive state] An equivalence class of histories that gives the same
 distribution for every contemplated future experiment. This is the
 operational state available in principle to the agent.
 \item[Goal-progress state] The state of a counter or deterministic finite
 automaton (DFA) used to recognize a sequence goal. It can be useful memory,
 but it need not be encoded by the quantum system at all.
\end{description}
Conflating these spaces makes a low-dimensional result vacuous. A classical
DFA with nine nodes can be attached to an untouched qubit and can reproduce
any desired $3\times3$ graph. The graph then lives in the task designer's
counter, not in controlled quantum experience. Conversely, nonorthogonal
quantum states can carry a large orbit even when no one-shot measurement can
name its members without error. We use null-instrument and memory-accounting
controls below to distinguish these cases.

\subsection{What low dimension really forbids}

Suppose $N$ density operators $\rho_i$ must be identified with certainty by
one common POVM $\{E_j\}_{j=1}^N$. Perfect identification means
\begin{equation}
 \Tr(\rho_iE_j)=\delta_{ij},\qquad E_j\succeq0,
 \qquad\sum_jE_j=I. \label{eq:perfect-discrimination}
\end{equation}
For pure states $\rho_i=|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|$, positivity and
$0\preceq E_i\preceq I$ imply
$E_i|\psi_i\rangle=|\psi_i\rangle$ and
$E_i|\psi_j\rangle=0$ for $j\ne i$. Hence
\begin{equation}
 \langle\psi_j|\psi_i\rangle
 =\langle\psi_j|E_i|\psi_i\rangle=0.
\end{equation}
The mixed-state statement is the same at the level of supports: perfectly
discriminable states have mutually orthogonal supports. Therefore
\begin{equation}
 N\le d \label{eq:one-shot-bound}
\end{equation}
whenever ``place'' means an exclusively recognizable one-shot state in
$\mathbb C^d$. An adaptive measurement protocol on a \emph{single} copy does
not evade the bound; the complete outcome tree is itself a POVM. Repeated
preparations, persistent action history, or an external recognizer change the
premise and can evade it.

This proposition explains both the value and limitation of the earlier
nine-level construction. It realizes the strongest classical-place
interpretation. The constructions below instead use nonorthogonal endpoints,
covariant measurement events, or action histories. They answer a different
and more agent-centered question.

\subsection{A finite necessary-and-sufficient criterion}

Before constructing examples, it helps to state exactly what must be true of a
finite cost table. Let $S$ be a finite predictive-state set, $g\in S$ an
absorbing goal, $P^a_{ij}$ the outcome-marginal transition probability under
action $a$, and $c(i,a)>0$ its cost. A proposed directed difficulty
$D(i,g)$ is optimal stochastic-shortest-path cost if and only if it satisfies
the Bellman equations
\begin{align}
 D(g,g)&=0,\label{eq:lowdim-bellman-goal}\\
 D(i,g)&=\min_a\left[c(i,a)+\sum_jP^a_{ij}D(j,g)\right]
 \quad(i\ne g), \label{eq:lowdim-bellman}
\end{align}
with at least one proper minimizing policy that reaches $g$ almost surely.
This is not merely an algorithm. The equalities certify that no omitted
strategy is cheaper, while properness excludes an attractive zero-progress
cycle. They are the dynamic-programming half of an exact characterization
\cite{bellman1957,bertsekas1991,puterman1994}.

Ordinary Euclidean space imposes a second, independent condition. Symmetrize
the relevant costs if reversibility is part of the hypothesis and form the
matrix $D=[D_{ij}]$. With
\begin{equation}
 J=I-\frac1N\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top,
 \qquad B=-\frac12J(D\circ D)J, \label{eq:schoenberg-gram}
\end{equation}
where $\circ$ is elementwise multiplication, Schoenberg's theorem states
\begin{equation}
 D_{ij}=\|x_i-x_j\|_2\text{ for some }x_i\in\mathbb R^k
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 B\succeq0\text{ and }\operatorname{rank}B\le k
 \label{eq:schoenberg-criterion}
\end{equation}
for a symmetric hollow distance matrix \cite{schoenberg1935,gower1966}.
The coordinates are recovered from the positive eigendecomposition
$B=X X^\top$. Thus Bellman realizability plus the positive-semidefinite rank
criterion is necessary and sufficient for a finite \emph{cost matrix} to be
exactly Euclidean.

It is not yet sufficient for a persuasive emergent space. A task designer can
hard-code $D$ into an action price or goal automaton. Physical emergence also
requires translated goal semantics, locally reusable actions, predictive
distinguishability, trajectories, robustness to finite resolution, and an
audit of every nonquantum memory variable. These are causal and operational
conditions layered on top of the mathematical criterion.

\subsection{Exact qubit word geometry}

Take the equatorial state and two commuting phase controls
\begin{equation}
 |+\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2},\qquad
 U=\diag(1,e^{i\alpha}),\qquad V=\diag(1,e^{i\beta}),
 \label{eq:qubit-phase-controls}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
 \alpha=\frac{2\pi}{9}+\epsilon\sqrt2,qquad
 \beta=\frac{2\pi}{3}+\epsilon\sqrt3,qquad
 \epsilon=10^{-2}. \label{eq:irrational-phases}
\end{equation}
The four unit-cost cardinal instruments are the one-Kraus channels
$U,U^\dagger,V,V^\dagger$. Define nine goals by
\begin{equation}
 |\psi_{ij}\rangle=U^iV^j|+\rangle,qquad i,j\in\{0,1,2\}.
 \label{eq:qubit-nine-goals}
\end{equation}
All nine are nonorthogonal rays of a single qubit.

The essential algebra is faithfulness. If
$U^aV^b|+\rangle\sim|+\rangle$, equality of relative phase requires
\begin{equation}
 a\alpha+b\beta=2\pi m,qquad a,b,m\in\mathbb Z. \label{eq:phase-relation}
\end{equation}
After substitution,
\begin{equation}
 2\pi\left(\frac a9+\frac b3-m\right)
 +\epsilon(a\sqrt2+b\sqrt3)=0. \label{eq:faithfulness-proof}
\end{equation}
If the rational coefficient of $\pi$ were nonzero, this equality would make
$\pi$ algebraic. It must therefore vanish. Linear independence of
$\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt3$ over the rationals then gives $a=b=0$. The projective
stabilizer is trivial, so the qubit orbit carries a faithful action of
$\mathbb Z^2$.

Because $U$ and $V$ commute, every action word reduces to $U^aV^b$.
Faithfulness forces the unique displacement from $(i,j)$ to $(k,l)$ to be
$(k-i,l-j)$. Cardinal moves attain the lower bound, proving
\begin{equation}
 d_1((i,j),(k,l))=|k-i|+|l-j|. \label{eq:qubit-manhattan}
\end{equation}
This is exactly the open $3\times3$ square-lattice word metric. In the
all-pairs calculation, maximum terminal infidelity was
$6.7\times10^{-16}$.

The same goals can be stated as sequence languages: accept action histories
whose signed $U$ and $V$ exponent totals equal the desired displacement. At a
fixed horizon this is a regular language recognized by a finite counter DFA.
Without a horizon, two unbounded integer counters are required, so it would be
incorrect to call the exact infinite language regular. This bookkeeping detail
matters because an unbounded recognizer can silently supply more state than the
qubit.

\subsection{From Manhattan words to exact Euclidean waiting time}

The graph distance in \cref{eq:qubit-manhattan} is $L^1$, not ordinary
$L^2$ distance. To obtain exact Euclidean cost while retaining a common unit
price, provide a binary retry instrument for every nonzero patch displacement
$\delta=(a,b)$. Let $r=\|\delta\|_2$ and
\begin{equation}
 K_{\rm s}(\delta)=\sqrt{\frac1r}\,U^aV^b,qquad
 K_{\rm f}(\delta)=\sqrt{1-\frac1r}\,I. \label{eq:retry-kraus}
\end{equation}
Nonzero integer displacements have $r\ge1$, and
$K_{\rm s}^\dagger K_{\rm s}+K_{\rm f}^\dagger K_{\rm f}=I$. Failure leaves
the conditional state unchanged; success performs the exact translation.
Repeated attempts have geometric waiting time
\begin{equation}
 \E T_\delta=\frac1{p_\delta}=r. \label{eq:retry-mean}
\end{equation}

A direct attempt therefore achieves ordinary Euclidean distance in expected
unit interventions. It is optimal: any successful decomposition
$\delta=\delta_1+\cdots+\delta_n$ costs in expectation
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{q=1}^n\|\delta_q\|_2
 \ge\left\|\sum_{q=1}^n\delta_q\right\|_2
 =\|\delta\|_2, \label{eq:retry-triangle}
\end{equation}
by the triangle inequality. Equivalently, substituting
$D(x,g)=\|g-x\|_2$ into \cref{eq:lowdim-bellman} makes the direct
displacement action an equality and every composite alternative an
inequality. Fifty thousand samples for each of 24 nonzero displacements,
1.2 million attempts in aggregate, had maximum mean error 0.02897.

This is an exact theorem, but not yet a robust physical plane. The faithful
$\mathbb Z^2$ orbit is dense on one Bloch circle. Its inverse coordinate map is
discontinuous: arbitrarily distant words approach the same ray. The nine
projector tests are not exclusive; the worst false acceptance probability is
0.9025. When rays within infidelity $10^{-3}$ are treated as equivalent,
30.6\% of unordered patch pairs acquire a shortcut. The construction proves
that low Hilbert dimension does not preclude exact history geometry. It also
shows why algebraic faithfulness without finite-resolution stability is too
weak for ordinary space.

\subsection{A qutrit with a genuine two-dimensional phase manifold}

The qubit construction uses two integer windings on a one-dimensional circle.
A qutrit has two independent relative phases and can therefore carry a smooth
two-dimensional orbit. Choose
\begin{equation}
 |\psi_0\rangle=\sqrt{\frac38}|0\rangle+\frac12|1\rangle
 +\sqrt{\frac38}|2\rangle,
 \quad A=\diag(0,1,0),\quad B=\diag\left(0,\frac12,1\right).
 \label{eq:qutrit-phase-fiducial}
\end{equation}
For
$|\psi(\theta,\phi)\rangle=e^{i\theta A+i\phi B}|\psi_0\rangle$,
the Fubini--Study line element is the covariance matrix of the generators,
\begin{equation}
 ds^2=\operatorname{Var}(A)d\theta^2
 +2\operatorname{Cov}(A,B)d\theta d\phi
 +\operatorname{Var}(B)d\phi^2. \label{eq:fs-covariance}
\end{equation}
Direct evaluation gives
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Var}(A)=\operatorname{Var}(B)=\frac3{16},
 \qquad\operatorname{Cov}(A,B)=0. \label{eq:isotropic-fs}
\end{equation}
The quantum-state manifold is therefore locally flat and isotropic in these
coordinates, up to the common scale $3/16$.

To make a finite, robust orbit, set
\begin{equation}
 U=e^{4\pi iA/11},\qquad V=e^{4\pi iB/11}. \label{eq:qutrit-order11}
\end{equation}
Both have order 11. If $U^aV^b$ stabilizes the fiducial ray, the phase on the
$|0\rangle$ component fixes the global phase. The other two components give
$2a+b=0$ and $2b=0$ modulo 11, hence $a=b=0$. The 121 rays form a faithful
$\mathbb Z_{11}^2$ orbit. Because the nine selected goals use
$i,j\in\{0,1,2\}$, their difference set lies well inside the wrap radius.
They are a local chart rather than an attempted flattening of the full torus.

The same retry construction supplies exact Euclidean Bellman cost on this
chart. The numerical audit found a minimum pairwise orbit Frobenius separation
0.34505, Bellman residual $8.88\times10^{-16}$, exact distance error zero,
MDS reconstruction error $1.33\times10^{-15}$, and a Schoenberg spectrum with
exactly two positive eigenvalues, both six. Control distance correlates 0.98892
with trace distance. Monte Carlo waiting-time error was at most 0.02373.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{low_dimensional_hodology/results/qutrit-phase/qutrit_phase_lattice.png}
\caption{\textbf{Robust qutrit phase chart.} The generator covariance is
isotropic, the finite order-11 orbit has separated rays, and the selected
$3\times3$ patch has exact rank-two Euclidean expected cost. Procrustes-aligned
MDS coordinates coincide with the analytic chart.}
\label{fig:qutrit-phase-lattice}
\end{figure}

This qutrit model repairs the qubit's manifold-dimension and density defects,
but the Euclidean norm remains engineered into the retry probability
$p_\delta=1/\|\delta\|_2$. Moreover, providing every patch displacement as a
macro-instrument is nonlocal in action space. With only finitely many local
directions, shortest-path cost generically approaches a polygonal or Finsler
norm, not exact rotationally invariant $L^2$. Deriving the success law and a
continuum of effective directions from simpler microscopic physics is now the
central inverse-design problem.

\subsection{A fully operational qutrit goal: the Hesse SIC}

The phase chart still uses a synchronized history label to name its exact
endpoint. A complementary qutrit construction makes every goal an outcome of
one common physical measurement. Let
\begin{equation}
 X|j\rangle=|j+1\!\!\pmod3\rangle,qquad
 Z|j\rangle=\omega^j|j\rangle,qquad \omega=e^{2\pi i/3},
\end{equation}
and choose $|\psi_{00}\rangle=(0,1,-1)^\top/\sqrt2$. The orbit
\begin{equation}
 |\psi_{mn}\rangle=X^mZ^n|\psi_{00}\rangle
 \label{eq:hesse-orbit}
\end{equation}
is the qutrit Hesse SIC. Its projectors satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{m,n}\frac13\Pi_{mn}=I,qquad
 |\langle\psi_{mn}|\psi_{m'n'}\rangle|^2=\frac14
 \quad((m,n)\ne(m',n')), \label{eq:hesse-sic}
\end{equation}
as required for a symmetric informationally complete POVM
\cite{renes2004,scott2006}.

The physical repertoire contains $X^{\pm1},Z^{\pm1}$ and the common
measurement Kraus operators $K_{mn}=\Pi_{mn}/\sqrt3$. Outcome $(m,n)$ resets
the qutrit to the corresponding SIC state. Goal $g$ is the observable event
``the SIC measurement reports $g$,'' not a basis-state fidelity test. From
state $s$ its probability is $1/3$ if $s=g$ and $1/12$ otherwise.

The exact Bellman equation is
\begin{equation}
 V_g(s)=\min\left\{
 1+V_g(X^{\pm1}s),\ 1+V_g(Z^{\pm1}s),\
 1+\sum_{o\ne g}p(o|s)V_g(o)\right\}. \label{eq:hesse-bellman}
\end{equation}
It has the closed-form solution
\begin{equation}
 V_g(s)=6+d_T(s,g), \label{eq:hesse-solution}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
 d_T((m,n),(m',n'))=
 \min(|m-m'|,3-|m-m'|)+
 \min(|n-n'|,3-|n-n'|) \label{eq:torus-manhattan}
\end{equation}
is toroidal Manhattan distance. At the goal, one measurement is paid
immediately. The eight failed outcomes consist of four neighbors with value
seven and four distance-two states with value eight; their probability-weighted
continuation is $(4\cdot7+4\cdot8)/12=5$, giving $V_g(g)=6$. Away from the
goal, a shortest unitary translation reduces $d_T$ by one, while direct
substitution verifies the measurement inequality. The numerical Bellman
solver recovered the baseline and every excess cost, with Pearson and Spearman
correlation one.

This is the strongest operational low-dimensional example in the study:
states, reusable controls, common measurement, and goals are all quantum. It
also supplies an important negative result. The orbit is
$\mathbb Z_3\times\mathbb Z_3$, so it wraps. Sixteen of 36 unordered pairs
are shorter than their open-grid counterparts. Its double-centered Gram
matrix has four positive eigenvalues 3.5 and four negative eigenvalues $-1$;
raw 2D MDS stress is 0.383. It is an exact two-dimensional \emph{periodic
control topology}, not an exact Euclidean plane. Equiangularity also makes all
static off-diagonal trace distances identical: the geometry resides in
controlled transitions rather than state distinguishability.

\subsection{Elaborate sequence goals and memory provenance}

A second qutrit phase grid uses
\begin{equation}
 |\phi_{mn}\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle+\omega^m|1\rangle+\omega^n|2\rangle}{\sqrt3},
 \quad U=\diag(1,\omega,1),\quad V=\diag(1,1,\omega).
 \label{eq:qutrit-phase-grid}
\end{equation}
The nine covariant effects
$E_{mn}=|\phi_{mn}\rangle\langle\phi_{mn}|/3$ sum to identity. Its
baseline-subtracted hitting cost correlates 0.996116 with torus control
distance. Requiring $L=1,2,3$ \emph{consecutive} reports of the designated
outcome gives aligned baseline costs 5.588, 22.353, and 72.647 while preserving
the same 0.996116 correlation. The result cleanly separates where a translated
goal is from how demanding its internal completion condition is.

Yet the confirmation count is a goal-progress coordinate. To see why this
qualification is essential, we solved a deliberate null system: a nine-node
counter DFA coupled to identity controls on a qubit. It retains a perfect grid
while all nine conditional density operators are identical and every trace
distance is zero. The geometry is wholly external memory.

A more informative ablation compares the same two-counter goal under
independent fair coins and projective qubit $X/Z$ measurements. For the coin,
the exact cost surface is
\begin{equation}
 C_{\rm coin}=\begin{pmatrix}0&2&4\\2&4&6\\4&6&8\end{pmatrix};
 \end{equation}
for the qubit, measurement backaction yields
\begin{equation}
 C_{\rm qubit}=\begin{pmatrix}0&3&4\\3&5&6\\4&6&7\end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:counter-surfaces}
\end{equation}
The additive residual rises from numerical zero to one. Quantum dynamics has
warped an automaton-carried base, but it has not created the base. This is a
simple prototype of an internal physical fiber over an externally specified
spatial task.

\subsection{Approximate qubit planarity and the flatness--resolution trade-off}

There is also a less algebraic qubit route. The Bloch sphere is a smooth
two-dimensional manifold, so a sufficiently small patch is approximately
planar. We searched
\begin{equation}
 |\psi_{xy}\rangle=R_x(\theta)^xR_y(\theta)^y|0\rangle,
 \qquad x,y\in\{0,1,2\}, \label{eq:qubit-tangent-patch}
\end{equation}
over 180 angles. Subject to minimum trace separation 0.12, the selected
$\theta=0.309665$ radians gives Euclidean-distance correlation 0.989749,
2D MDS stress 0.007361, positive-Gram planarity 0.992494, mean translation
defect 0.009424, and commutator defect 0.010249.

flatness and resolvability must be reported together.

There is a sharper obstruction for exact ternary translation covariance. Two
commuting order-three qubit unitaries are simultaneously diagonalizable and,
after removing global phase, supply only one relative phase. A nontrivial Weyl
relation $XZ=\omega ZX$ gives
$\det(XZ)=\omega^d\det(ZX)$, so $\omega^d=1$ and $3$ divides $d$. This rules
out a faithful projective $\mathbb Z_3^2$ Weyl representation in dimension
two. It does not rule out the irrational qubit history encoding, nonunitary
instruments, or approximate local patches; it identifies exactly which
symmetry demand makes the qutrit minimal.

The answer to the motivating question is consequently conditional. A qubit
can carry an exact open $3\times3$ lattice as a faithful word/history metric,
and can approximate a resolved local plane in state space. It cannot provide a
robust two-relative-phase manifold or the exact ternary Weyl covariance of the
qutrit. A qutrit supports both a locally isotropic phase surface and a fully
operational covariant goal family. Finite-order symmetry naturally produces a
torus; open Euclidean space requires a local chart, a boundary mechanism, or an
infinite translation representation.

The experiments also reveal a hierarchy of increasingly strong claims:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item the goal recognizer has a two-dimensional graph;
 \item conditional quantum states vary in two independent directions;
 \item reusable controls act covariantly as two translations;
 \item optimal all-pairs costs satisfy the Bellman equations;
 \item the symmetrized costs pass the exact rank-two Schoenberg test;
 \item the construction survives finite resolution, noise, and memory
 ablation;
 \item learned trajectories, rather than privileged labels, localize the
 agent in the resulting chart.
\end{enumerate}
The present models reach different levels of this hierarchy. None yet derives
the Euclidean norm, large-scale locality, and robust operational localization
from a simple untuned quantum law. That gap is the most promising direction
for the next stage rather than a defect to hide.

\section{When the meaning of an action is itself learned}
\label{sec:informative-actions}

The preceding constructions still permit a subtle form of privileged
information. Even when an action is presented to the learner under an opaque
token, the scientific interpretation may inherit the fact that its matrix was
designed as a translation. A genuinely operational spatial claim must recover
that meaning from changes in observable probability laws.

\subsection{Predictive equivalence and two notions of information}

A \emph{test} is a contemplated future action string together with an outcome
event. Two histories are causally or predictively equivalent when every test
has the same conditional probability after either history. An operational
state is therefore an equivalence class of histories, as in a predictive state
representation~\cite{littman2001}. The meaning of an opaque action is the map
it induces on these predictive classes, not its source-code label.

Two senses of informativeness must be separated. Immediate information is
\begin{equation}
 I(S;O_t\mid A_t=a),
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the pre-action predictive class. Future operational information
is the distinguishability of later common-test laws with and without the
action. A one-outcome unitary has zero immediate information but may be fully
identifiable by later probes. Conversely, a destructive measurement can have
an informative outcome while erasing distinctions needed for spatial
composition.

This tension is principled. If every branch of an instrument maps a connected
nonorthogonality graph of pure states to the same prescribed translated rays
without conditional disturbance, linearity forces the branch amplitudes---and
hence outcome probabilities---to be state independent along each connected
component. Otherwise the classical outcome would reveal which nonorthogonal
input was present while leaving it undisturbed. This is the relevant
no-information-without-disturbance obstruction~\cite{fuchs1996,busch2009}; it
does not forbid informative motion, but it says that information must come
from later probes, backaction, or an additional memory system.

\subsection{A nonunitary qubit action chart is not yet a plane}

The minimal numerical experiment uses the reset state $|+x\rangle$ and opaque
binary instruments
\begin{equation}
 K_{b,o}=\sqrt{\frac{I+osX}{2}}R_z(\theta_b),
 \qquad o\in\{-1,+1\}.
\end{equation}
For hidden phases $\{\pm0.64,\pm2.02\}$ and strength $s=0.45$, immediate
outcomes and common terminal $X,Y,Z$ probes allow the learner to recover two
inverse button pairs, modulo the dihedral gauge of a square. Across 300 fixed
replications, chart recovery is 87.3\%, 98.7\%, and 100\% at two, five, and ten
samples per common test. With 300 samples per probe, predictive tomography has
mean trace-distance error 0.035.

The stronger quotient test fails. Among 41 external displacement classes made
from words of length at most four, 33 contain more than one word; none of those
33 has path-independent future-test statistics. The worst common-probe
discrepancy is 0.12849. Thus the qubit has an empirically learned two-axis
\emph{action chart}, but the putative lattice position still lives in a word
counter rather than in predictive equivalence classes of the quantum process.

\subsection{An exactly soluble informative qutrit topology}

Let $\{\Pi_o\}_{o=0}^8$ be the Hesse SIC and let
$U_a\in\{I,X,X^\dagger,Z,Z^\dagger\}$ be qutrit Weyl translations. Combine
motion and reporting into a single nine-outcome instrument,
\begin{equation}
 K_o^{(a)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\Pi_oU_a.
 \label{eq:integrated-hesse-instrument}
\end{equation}
Completeness follows from $\sum_o\Pi_o=3I$. If the pre-action class is the SIC
state $\Pi_s$, then
\begin{equation}
 P_a(o\mid s)=\frac13\Tr(\Pi_oU_a\Pi_sU_a^\dagger)
 =\begin{cases}
 1/3,&o=T_a(s),\\
 1/12,&o\ne T_a(s).
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The conditional branch state is exactly $\Pi_o$, so the last observed token is
a sufficient predictive state. With a uniform prior, one action carries
\begin{equation}
 \log_2 9-H(1/3,1/12,\ldots,1/12)=0.251629\ \text{bits}.
\end{equation}

Action IDs and outcome tokens were independently permuted before learning.
From 2,000 observations per source/action pair, all 45 maximum-likelihood token
images were recovered, with kernel mean absolute error 0.00505--0.00579. The
inferred permutations contain one identity and four order-three generators;
they commute, generate a group of order nine, and act transitively. Hence the
agent recovers a $\mathbb Z_3^2$ translation topology up to origin, axis, sign,
and token gauges, without receiving coordinates.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{low_dimensional_hodology/informative_actions/qutrit/figures/integrated_action_no_go.png}
\caption{\textbf{Informative topology is not sufficient for metric space.}
The integrated Hesse kernel has a learned translated peak and positive mutual
information. Weakening the measurement never separates self from edge. After

zero to a distinct neighbor, whereas a separated move/report benchmark retains
the toroidal graph levels.}
\label{fig:integrated-action-no-go}
\end{figure}

The exact Bellman audit is nevertheless negative. For the goal ``report token
$g$,'' every action costs one and
\begin{equation}
 V_g(s)=\min_a\left[1+\sum_{o\ne g}P_a(o\mid s)V_g(o)\right].
\end{equation}
Symmetry and direct substitution give $V_{\rm self}=V_{\rm edge}=4$ and
$V_{\rm diagonal}=5$. Subtracting the common self baseline therefore assigns
zero separation to a distinct nearest neighbor, violating identity of
indiscernibles. A continuous weakening from null reporting to the sharp
measure-and-prepare instrument preserves this equality. By contrast, separating
unitary movement from a SIC report gives the exact solution
$V_g(s)=6+d_T(s,g)$, but the movement action again has no informative outcome.

The combined result is a sharper research target, not a failed search. Exact
predictive action semantics exist in a qubit, and informative outcomes can
reveal exact two-dimensional translation topology in a qutrit. What remains is
to realize predictive separation, action identifiability, Bellman
realizability, metric validity, rank-two or rank-three Schoenberg structure,
and local compositionality in one instrument family. Outcome-conditioned,
group-covariant qutrit channels with non-rank-one effects and retained quantum
memory are the most promising next search space.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

Useful measurement strategies can be learned without exposing hidden quantum
variables. Recurrent memory sometimes helps, but a short literal history is a
strong small-world baseline. Goal embeddings carry moderate behavioral
meaning, although local policy geometry transfers more reliably than
whole-trajectory geometry. Directed coordinates expose coherent measurement
displacements even though scalar cost is poorly calibrated.

The spatial study gives a stronger but narrower positive statement: there
exists a valid quantum instrument/goal construction in which policy-derived
difficulty selects two dimensions, recovers a concealed lattice, and turns
observed action sequences into spatial paths. Its controls show that hidden
connectivity, coordinate recovery, control success, and Euclidean metric fit
are distinct requirements.

The predictive-atlas successor weakens the observation assumption
substantially. Exact online place is replaced by anticipated landmark
experience inferred from weak history. Near-oracle navigation and geometric
recovery show that a supplied current-state symbol is not necessary on this
world. The residual 2D-to-3D stress improvement is equally informative:
representation error appears as nonspatial metric distortion, making emergent
dimension a joint property of dynamics, sensing, memory, and control.

The active successor adds rational selective observation. Reversible coherent
movement closes the boundary-homing loophole, and adaptive sensing recovers
most fixed-scan competence at less than 40\% of its total intervention cost.
The gap between goal-relative and atlas-preserving policies shows that local
decision sufficiency and global geometric coherence are different
representation criteria. Separating sensing from movement also provides the
first empirical reason to model full hodological state as structured over a
spatial base rather than forcing all difficulty into one metric.

The low-dimensional study removes a different hidden assumption. Nine goals
do not require nine orthogonal states. The qubit gives a minimal exact
algebraic encoding, the qutrit gives a robust two-phase manifold, and the
Hesse-SIC instrument gives a fully operational analytic goal family. Their
different failures are as informative as their successes. The qubit's exact
coordinates are unstable at finite resolution; the finite qutrit Weyl orbit
wraps into a torus; and the exact Euclidean retry models place the desired norm
in the success law. ``Two-dimensional'' must therefore specify whether it
refers to a control algebra, a state manifold, a graph topology, or a Euclidean
distance matrix.

These results do not show that a Bloch sphere, Hilbert space, or uniquely
quantum geometry spontaneously emerged. Such a claim would require latent-state
analysis, intervention ablations, classical controls, and privileged offline
decoding of quantum invariants. Other limitations are:
\begin{itemize}
 \item two seeds cannot support significance claims; modern RL methodology
 recommends more runs, intervals, robust aggregates, and performance profiles
 \cite{agarwal2021};
 \item episode budgets are equal, not compute budgets, and hyperparameters are
 not independently optimized;
 \item horizon 15 censors hard goals, reward shaping affects behavior, and
 conditional steps omit failures;
 \item one-step prediction does not guarantee predictive sufficiency;
 \item goal embeddings are lookup entries and cannot represent unseen goals;
 \item embedding targets come from the same Q-head, creating shared-error and
 circularity risks despite held-out evaluation;
 \item cost is learned off-policy from reward-oriented behavior and is not
 constrained to satisfy stochastic-shortest-path consistency;
 \item simulators omit Hamiltonian dynamics, decoherence, detector error,
 latency, and hardware drift.
 \item the spatial construction uses localized qudit states and
 entanglement-breaking measure-and-prepare channels; latent places, open
 boundaries, and a sharp localization probe are designed into the simulator;
 \item the $3\times3$ lattice is too small to establish continuum structure,
 topology recovery, scale dependence, or curvature, and three spatial seeds
 remain exploratory;
 \item symmetrizing learned hitting costs is appropriate for testing a
 Euclidean hypothesis but discards directed information that a more general
 hodological geometry must retain.
 \item the predictive atlas uses delayed landmark supervision and
 landmark-anchored transition surveys; it does not discover its place
 vocabulary without labels;
 \item its diagonal QND beacons commute with the latent place basis and its
 movement channels remain entanglement breaking, so no specifically coherent
 quantum resource is responsible for localization;
 \item every navigation episode pays for a fixed 48-probe scan, which is
 omitted from relative movement geometry but dominates the reported total
 intervention count;
 \item the fixed predictive controller's belief-weighted state costs
 approximate rather than solve the full belief-MDP, and that experiment does
 not actively choose additional sensing;
 \item the active study repairs the last limitation only with tabular,
 landmark-anchored models, a depth-three sensing tree or hand-set 0.95
 confidence constraint, and three seeds;
 \item reversible layer swaps are coherent and doubly stochastic but were
 deliberately designed; their contextual action semantics are not ordinary
 global translations;
 \item the entropy height is an epistemic visualization, not a demonstrated
 physical fiber, local trivialization, connection, or holonomy.
 \item the exact qubit $\mathbb Z^2$ representation is dense on a Bloch circle,
 so its coordinate inverse is discontinuous and its finite-tolerance geometry
 is not robust;
 \item no common one-shot measurement can exclusively identify the nine
 nonorthogonal qubit goals, and sequence recognition requires explicitly
 audited history memory;
 \item the qutrit phase chart is finite and locally isotropic, but exact
 Euclidean cost is imposed through inverse-distance retry probabilities and a
 catalog of displacement macro-actions;
 \item the Hesse-SIC construction is exact and operational but periodic: its
 $\mathbb Z_3^2$ shortest-path metric fails the rank-two Euclidean criterion;
 \item sequence-confirmation goals introduce a progress automaton whose state
 must not be mistaken for a quantum spatial coordinate.
\end{itemize}

\section{Outlook}
\label{sec:outlook}

\paragraph{Compositional goals and transfer.}
Encode checkpoint sequences with a GRU or transformer, hold out complete goals,
and test zero/few-shot transfer. Geometry matters if proximity predicts
adaptation speed. Successor features provide a principled comparison
\cite{barreto2017}.

\paragraph{Multi-step predictive state.}
Train action-conditional predictions over entire future tests, compare
recurrent and transformer memory, and measure predictive calibration by
horizon. Vary informational completeness to determine which latent dimensions
disappear.

\paragraph{Planning in possibility space.}
Use \cref{eq:planning} to compare direct Q-control, greedy distance reduction,
and tree search through imagined outcomes. The learned geometry should improve
decisions, not only visualization.

\paragraph{Distributional reachability.}
Learn $\tau_g$ distributions or $R_g(z,T)$ directly, using proper scoring rules
and reliability plots. Distributional Bellman methods are a starting point
\cite{bellemare2017}.

\paragraph{Directed non-Euclidean structure.}
Explore directed graphs, asymmetric energy models, quasimetrics, hyperbolic
goal hierarchies, and Finsler-like local costs. Choose geometry through
predictive validity and planning utility, not aesthetics.

\paragraph{Derive, rather than prescribe, the Euclidean norm.}
This experiment has now been performed for fixed local qutrit control
catalogs. If translation $a$ has effective retry cost $c_a$, the Bellman
distance is a weighted word metric whose stable unit ball is
\begin{equation}
 B_{\rm vel}=\operatorname{conv}\{a/c_a\}.
 \label{eq:velocity-hull}
\end{equation}
A finite catalog therefore has a polygonal unit ball and cannot globally equal
the circular Euclidean norm. With maximum angular gap $\Delta_{\max}$, its
worst asymptotic directional excess is
$\sec(\Delta_{\max}/2)-1$; approximately uniform $K$-direction families
converge as $O(K^{-2})$ but retain a nonzero floor at fixed $K$.

The deterministic experiment fits only radius-four displacements and tests
through radius twelve. Held-out Euclidean relative RMSE falls from 30.03\%
with four axial directions to 3.85\% with eight and 1.62\% with 32. The
32-direction metric has 2D MDS stress 0.0289, but its Schoenberg negative
eigenmass remains 0.082. Sixteen nominal directions do no better than eight
because the added knight actions are Bellman-dominated. Thus the experiment
both removes the source--target lookup table and proves why a fixed finite
alphabet remains Finsler rather than exactly Riemannian.

The stronger next step is continuous qutrit control with quadratic effort,
$\frac12u^TR(x)u$. A constant positive-definite $R$ induces a flat
ellipsoidal infinitesimal metric without installing one macro-action per
displacement. Position-dependent $R(x)$ then gives a controlled route to
curvature. The open learning question is whether rotational symmetry and the
control tensor can be inferred from weak histories rather than supplied.

\paragraph{Scale the exact analytic benchmark.}
Use the Hesse-SIC Bellman solution as a unit test for learned solvers, then
replace $\mathbb Z_3^2$ by larger odd-order phase orbits. Compare local charts,
periodic topology, and open-boundary constructions with the same
Bellman--Schoenberg diagnostics. The desired finite-size signature is a stable
two-eigenvalue positive Gram sector over increasing radii before boundary or
wrap corrections appear.

\paragraph{Optimize states, instruments, and landmarks.}
Generalize \cref{eq:bilevel} from one success probability to positive Choi
matrices or Stinespring isometries, enforcing complete positivity and trace
preservation. Jointly select goal landmarks for coverage and stable
trilateration, penalizing nonlocal teleportation and goal-specific actions.
Evolutionary search is a robust first outer loop; differentiable meta-learning
becomes attractive once the inner planner is model-based.

\paragraph{From predictive to active localization.}
Explicit online place localization was removed in
\cref{sec:predictive-atlas}, and sensing now competes with movement and
commitment in \cref{sec:active-atlas}. The next agent should learn its
observation and transition models jointly from uninterrupted experience,
eliminating separated landmark-anchored surveys, and solve the continuous
belief problem with point-based planning, tree search, or a learned
distributional value. Scaling from reversible grids to tori, cylinders,
spheres, and defected graphs should be evaluated with out-of-sample stress,
local dimension, neighborhood preservation, and topology, not only global MDS
\cite{tenenbaum2000}.

\paragraph{Spatial base and internal fiber.}
The low-dimensional hidden-start benchmark now makes the need for this split
exact. For nine nonorthogonal qutrit phase preparations and a strength-$\eta$
covariant sensor, a single outcome-directed move has
\begin{equation}
 P(\widehat S=S)=\frac{1+2\eta}{9},\qquad
 F_{\rm target}=\frac{1+2\eta}{3}.
 \label{eq:localization-preparation-gap}
\end{equation}
At $\eta=1$, measurement prepares a state that can be routed with unit
fidelity while identifying the original preparation only one third of the
time; subsequent sharp outcomes cannot recover erased origin information.
Across 60,000 seeded episodes, a present-predictive-state controller greatly
outperforms origin-label movement after repeated strong sensing. Predictive
likelihood, posterior calibration, and current-state control must therefore be
evaluated separately.

Give each place several internal quantum states and goals, then test whether
the full hodological state space $E$ locally decomposes as
$F_b\hookrightarrow E\xrightarrow{\pi}B$, with a stable 2D or 3D spatial base
$B$ and internal fiber $F_b$ \cite{steenrod1951}. Actions may be horizontal,
vertical, or coupled. Learned transition maps between local charts and the
failure of an internal state to return after transport around a closed base
loop provide operational notions of connection and holonomy.

\paragraph{Curvature and spacetime.}
Position-dependent transition kernels permit local metric and transport
curvature tests; Ollivier curvature supplies one principled discrete diagnostic
for Markov processes \cite{ollivier2009}. This is only a bridge. General
relativity relates spacetime curvature to dynamics \cite{einstein1916}, so a
credible analogue would require robust local geometry, connection, sources,
and an independently tested field relation. Time cannot simply be appended as
a Euclidean coordinate: future-goal reachability must first yield causal order,
directed cones, clock tasks, and observer-dependent simultaneity.

\paragraph{Information-seeking action.}
The active atlas already replaces fixed observation with expected information
gain and goal-relative decision risk. The next step is information-seeking
\emph{exploration}: choose experiments that reduce uncertainty in the jointly
learned dynamics, reachability, and chart structure, not merely the current
place posterior.

\paragraph{Privileged offline physics.}
Pair learned $z_t$ with hidden Bloch coordinates only after training. Test
decodability, intrinsic dimension, convexity, and equivalence classes; compare
against classical partially observed environments.

\paragraph{Stronger statistics and hardware.}
Use 10--20 seeds, longer budgets, bootstrap intervals, interquartile means, and
performance profiles \cite{henderson2018,agarwal2021}. Then add decoherence,
readout error, continuous weak measurement, and device drift, ultimately
learning from laboratory records as envisioned in model-free quantum control
\cite{sivak2022}.

\section{Conclusion}

An agent beginning only with interventions and outcomes can learn more than a
policy. It can compress history, predict contemplated measurements, compare
aims by behavior, estimate goal-relative reachability, and represent
measurement backaction as movement through future possibility. The present
study makes that program concrete but also establishes a demanding standard:
an attractive embedding is not self-validating. It must predict independent
behavior, transfer, and reachability.

The inverse-designed qudit world additionally establishes the first spatial
existence result: all-pairs learned difficulty robustly prefers two dimensions
and learned policies trace paths in that emergent map. Just as importantly,
the failures show that a single-origin task, a hidden grid, or a visually
grid-like embedding is not enough. Broad source coverage, operational
localization or memory, and approximately isotropic movement costs are jointly
required.

The predictive atlas advances that result from reported place to inferred
place. Weak, individually ambiguous quantum beacons are integrated into a
distribution over future landmark experiences; blind movement reports update
that distribution; and belief-state policies recover a two-dimensional
goal-cost map with 97.3\% all-pairs success. The short-memory and null-sensor
controls show that the map depends on accumulated operational evidence rather
than network capacity or a hidden grid alone. Space in this construction is
therefore closer to a learned organization of action and anticipation, though
delayed labels and a designed place basis remain.

The active atlas makes observation itself part of goal-directed behavior.
Coherent reversible movements prevent a blind policy from synchronizing an
unknown ensemble, and learned active sensing retains 96.5\% all-pairs success
while reducing fixed-scan cost by 62.1\%. The cheaper pragmatic state is less
geometrically coherent than the full landmark posterior, demonstrating that a
common space demands more information than one locally adequate decision.
Movement and sensing costs separate into a spatial base and an epistemic
internal coordinate, providing a concrete but provisional bridge toward the
project's fiber-bundle program.

The low-dimensional results show that this program does not fundamentally
depend on a classical nine-state register. A qubit already supports an exact
open lattice word metric, while a qutrit supports a resolved two-phase surface
and common covariant outcome goals. At the same time, exact solvability exposes
the central unresolved mechanism: a faithful history code need not be robust,
a two-generator torus need not be Euclidean, and an exact Euclidean cost can be
put in by hand through action probabilities. Bellman consistency and the
Schoenberg rank test now provide proof-level acceptance criteria. The next
scientific objective is to make a local, noise-stable Euclidean metric survive
held-out scales and directions under a small physically natural Kraus family.

The geometry-aware agent is competitive but not universally superior. Its
embedding has measurable, seed-dependent strategic meaning; its directed cost
orders some goals but is not accurate in absolute units. These shortcomings
identify the next experiments. The long-term prospect is an agent not handed a
physical state space, but able to construct a predictive present and a geometry
of possible aims from action and consequence alone.

\section{Worked examples for the new reader}

\subsection{A numerical Q-learning update}

Suppose the current estimate for an observed state--action pair is
$Q(s,a)=2.0$. The action gives reward $r=1$, the next state is nonterminal, the
largest next-state action value is 4, and $\gamma=0.95$. The TD target and error
are
\begin{equation}
 Y^Q=1+0.95(4)=4.8,
 \qquad \delta^Q=4.8-2.0=2.8.
\end{equation}
With $\alpha=0.1$, the updated entry is
\begin{equation}
 Q_{\rm new}(s,a)=2.0+0.1(2.8)=2.28.
\end{equation}
The target is not treated as certain: one sample moves the estimate only ten
percent of the way. Repeated transitions average stochastic outcomes. If the
transition terminated, the next-state term would be removed and the target
would be just the observed reward.

\subsection{Reward value versus intervention cost}

Consider two policies for the same goal. Policy A succeeds in two steps with
probability one. Policy B succeeds in one step with probability $0.6$ and in
ten steps otherwise. With only a large completion reward and discounting,
policy preference depends on $\gamma$ and reward design. Their expected
undiscounted hitting costs are unambiguous:
\begin{equation}
 \E\tau_A=2,
 \qquad
 \E\tau_B=0.6(1)+0.4(10)=4.6.
\end{equation}
At deadline $T=1$, however, B has success probability 0.6 while A has zero.
This example explains why expected cost, discounted reward, and finite-time
success are related but distinct descriptions of goal difficulty.

\subsection{Why a square is intrinsically two-dimensional}

Take four ideal points at $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)$. Their Euclidean distance
matrix is
\begin{equation}
 D=\begin{pmatrix}
 0&1&1&\sqrt2\\
 1&0&\sqrt2&1\\
 1&\sqrt2&0&1\\
 \sqrt2&1&1&0
 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Double centering $D^{\circ2}$ using \cref{eq:double-centering} yields a
positive-semidefinite Gram matrix of rank two. Consequently classical MDS has
two positive eigenvalues and reconstructs the square exactly in $\R^2$; a
one-dimensional reconstruction must distort at least one pair. If the
diagonal entries were changed from $\sqrt2$ to 1 while axial entries remained
1, the relation would no longer describe the ordinary unit square even though
a plotting algorithm might still arrange the four labels in a square-like
pattern. This miniature example is the logic behind the diagonal-cost design
and the separation of Procrustes recovery from stress.

\subsection{Spatial experiment as an auditable algorithm}

The complete study can be summarized without implementation-specific detail:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item For every candidate $p_d$, construct the valid movement instruments,
 compute exact all-pairs stochastic shortest paths, and score their 2D
 Euclidean compatibility.
 \item Fix the best candidate before training. For each random seed and each
 control world, initialize an empty Q table.
 \item Repeatedly sample a source and goal uniformly, emit the coordinate-free
 source symbol, and train by epsilon-greedy Q-learning for at most 12 actions.
 \item Freeze the policy. For each of 81 ordered source--goal pairs, run 100
 greedy trials and record success and restricted hitting cost.
 \item Subtract the common terminal-probe overhead, retain ordered asymmetry,
 and symmetrize costs only for the Euclidean test.
 \item Fit 1D, 2D, and 3D MDS coordinates; calculate stress, spectrum,
 calibration, directionality, and privileged Procrustes diagnostics.
 \item Map fresh observed policy trajectories into coordinates inferred from
 the all-pairs costs. Never use concealed coordinates to select an action.
\end{enumerate}
This ordering prevents test information from entering the policy and makes
each transformation from quantum instrument to geometric claim inspectable.
